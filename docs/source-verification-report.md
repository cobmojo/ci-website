# Source verification report

What was checked, what was qualified, what was withdrawn, and what still needs
a specialist. Reviewed 29 July 2026.

The source document is the canonical statement of what the author is arguing.
It is not automatically authoritative for historical, linguistic or statistical
claims. Where the evidence supports a narrower claim than the source makes, the
site states the narrower claim and records the change publicly.

---

## 1. Claims withdrawn

These appeared in the source document and are **not** repeated on the site.

### Gehenna as a burning rubbish dump
**Source:** "In Jesus' day, Gehenna was a place where trash and dead bodies
were burned."

**Finding:** No source from the period describes a perpetually burning refuse
dump in the Valley of Hinnom. The tradition is widely regarded as a much later
inference. Repeating it would be asserting a colourful detail with nothing
behind it.

**Treatment:** Withdrawn. S15 keeps everything that *is* attested: the real
valley, child sacrifice under Ahaz and Manasseh, Jeremiah renaming it the Valley
of Slaughter, unburied corpses, and the prophetic judgment imagery Jesus draws
on. A caution callout explains the uncertainty.

### Satan's motive for promoting eternal torment
**Source:** the only sustained physical torment in the Old Testament is
inflicted on Job by Satan, therefore Satan wants people to believe God punishes
by torment.

**Finding:** Speculative, and it attributes the sincere convictions of other
Christians to demonic deception.

**Treatment:** Removed entirely from the public site. Recorded in the migration
ledger as a personal reflection retained in the private source record only.

### The specific heaven-to-hell ratio
**Source:** Jesus spoke about heaven at least three times as often as hell.

**Finding:** The figure comes from a single popular article that publishes no
verse list, no definition of what counts as a reference to hell, no decision on
whether kingdom language counts as heaven, and no rule for Gospel parallels or
repeated sayings. The author recorded in his own notes that he did not
reproduce the count.

**Treatment:** The ratio is not asserted. S34 states only that the familiar
claim is not well supported, and sets out exactly what a defensible count would
require. The section carries a `revision-needed` status until one exists.

### The proportion of near-death experiences that are positive
**Source:** most such experiences are positive and portray a universalist
afterlife.

**Finding:** No evidence base cited, and the argument does not need it.

**Treatment:** Rewritten in conditional form: to the extent that reported
experiences of heaven are set aside for conflicting with Scripture, consistency
requires the same treatment of reported experiences of hell. A callout names the
withdrawn quantitative claim.

### The speculative aside on angels and time
**Source:** explicitly labelled by the author as a "wild random thought without
scriptural support."

**Treatment:** Not reproduced on any page, and excluded from summaries,
metadata and the case map. Its existence is recorded in the ledger.

## 2. Claims narrowed

### The early-church majority
**Source:** conditional immortality was "apparently the majority view until at
least AD 300."

**Finding:** The surviving corpus is small, fragmentary and largely indirect.
Several figures leave nothing usable on final punishment. A counted majority is
not demonstrable in either direction.

**Treatment:** RB1 states the narrower, defensible claim: eternal conscious
torment was not the universal or uncontested position of the earliest
centuries, and identifiable early writers describe the end of the unrighteous
as the loss of continued life. The evidence table classifies each figure as
clear, probable, disputed or **indeterminate**, and the blank rows from the
source table are marked indeterminate rather than being assigned to a side.

### Augustine's role
**Source:** Augustine "established ECT as the predominant view."

**Treatment:** Presented as a decisive influence within a longer development,
with the page noting that naming one writer as the cause compresses a process
into an event. Augustine's own remark that very many in his day denied eternal
torment is retained with its full locator.

### Plato and the immortal soul
**Source:** "The immortality of the soul is from Plato, not the Bible!"

**Treatment:** Split. The biblical claim (Scripture attributes immortality to
God and describes it as a gift) is kept in full and argued from the text. The
historical claim is kept but qualified: the development involves Second Temple
Jewish anthropology and centuries of Christian reflection, not one line of
influence, and the bodily resurrection was defended *against* philosophical
accounts of the soul.

### "The majority of people ever created"
Attributed to the author's reading of Matthew 7:13-14 and flagged as contested,
rather than asserted as fact.

## 3. Corrections accepted from the source's own review thread

### The Aquinas quotation
The passage quoted for infinite punishment is stated by Aquinas as an
**objection he then answers**. Raised by a reviewer, accepted by the author at
the time. RB3 now gives the article and objection number and notes that the
circulating English wording is a popular variant rather than a standard
translation.

### Edom and literal smoke
A reviewer asked whether Edom's destruction is past, since if so the smoke
rising for ever was never literal. The author agreed. S02 now makes this
explicit, which strengthens the parallel rather than weakening it.

### The eternal-life inconsistency
A reviewer noted that interpreters who argue from John 17:3 that eternal life
means a *quality* of life often insist that eternal punishment means *duration*.
Incorporated into S04.

## 4. Errors of citation corrected

- **"Image of the invisible God"** is Colossians 1:15. The source attributes it
  to 2 Corinthians 4:4. S31 names both references correctly.
- **The narrow-way saying** is Matthew 7:13-14. The source cites Matthew 7:13-24
  in Appendix 2. Corrected, with the correction noted on the page.
- **Sections 17 and 18** are reversed between the source's table of contents and
  its body headings. The body is canonical: S17 is Jesus' death, S18 is physical
  death as a parallel. Two internal cross-references in the source point at the
  wrong number as a result; they are not reproduced. A build check prevents this
  reverting.

## 6. Where the author's own uncertainty is preserved

The site does not tidy away hesitancy.

- **The beast and the false prophet** (S03, S32): the author's reading of these
  as spiritual entities is marked as his present interpretation, with his own
  statement that he finds Revelation difficult here and is open to correction.
  The alternative conditionalist reading, that they are human and are destroyed,
  is stated fairly.
- **Satan and the fallen angels** (S32): marked
  `alternative-conditionalist-reading`. Conditionalists genuinely divide here,
  and the page says the human case does not depend on resolving it.
- **"Eternal sin"** (RB3, S04): the author records doubt about how much this
  contributes. It appears as a supporting observation, never as a decisive step.
- **Revelation 20 placement** (S03): stated plainly as an argument from silence,
  suggestive rather than decisive.
- **The age of accountability**: the source contains a note wondering whether to
  address it, and no developed argument. It is recorded as an open research
  question, not invented into a page.

## 7. Method for historical claims

1. Prefer a primary text over any modern summary of it.
2. Where only a modern discussion is available, name it as the source of the
   claim rather than asserting the claim directly.
3. Do not use a conditionalist site as the sole account of what defenders of
   eternal conscious torment believe. MacArthur and Aquinas are quoted from
   their own words.
4. Do not claim scholarly consensus. No page on this site does.
5. Distinguish clear, probable, disputed and indeterminate evidence, and say
   which is which.

## 8. Method for original languages

1. Show the original text with a `lang` attribute, a transliteration and a gloss,
   so a reader who does not read the script can still follow the argument.
2. State the standard lexical range before making any contextual argument.
3. Never imply that a word's root determines its meaning.
4. Never let a concordance count stand in for exegesis in context.
5. State competing interpretations as real alternatives.
6. Omit a morphological parsing rather than guess one.
7. Mark the page for specialist review whenever the argument depends on the
   language claim.
