## In brief

The argument asks a single question: if you added together everything every
human being will ultimately experience, what would the total be?

It sets out four quantities, proposes an equation relating them, and then runs
the equation twice, once on the assumptions of eternal conscious torment and
once on the assumptions of conditional immortality. The two runs give opposite
signs. Whether that means anything is a separate matter, taken up at the end.

## The proposed equation

In words:

The number in hell multiplied by the value of hell, plus the number in heaven
multiplied by the value of heaven, equals the net outcome of humanity.

In shorter form:

(Number in hell × Value of hell) + (Number in heaven × Value of heaven) = Net
outcome of humanity

What that means in ordinary language is simply this: all the goodness
experienced in heaven, added to all the badness experienced in hell, gives the
net result of the whole human experiment.

## The premise about the numbers

One further premise is needed before the equation can be run, and it comes from
Jesus.

<Scripture reference="Matthew 7:13-14" />

Few find the narrow way that leads to life; many take the broad road that leads
to destruction. Taken at face value that means the number in heaven is smaller
than the number in hell.

## Case one: the assumptions of eternal conscious torment

On a strongly symmetrical version of the traditional view, hell is the equal
and opposite of heaven: a negative experience of unbounded duration set against
a positive one.

So the value of heaven is positive infinity, and the value of hell is negative
infinity, which is to say it is the value of heaven multiplied by minus one.

Substituting that into the equation, the two terms can be gathered together,
and what is left is the difference between the two head counts multiplied by
the value of heaven:

(Number in heaven minus Number in hell) × Value of heaven = Net outcome of
humanity

Since the number in heaven is the smaller of the two, the bracket is negative.
Multiplied by an unbounded positive quantity, the result is negative without
bound. On this branch, the net outcome of humanity is negative infinity.

<Callout tone="note" title="Not every defender of the traditional view accepts this premise" as="h3">
The branch above depends on treating hell as the exact mirror of heaven. Many
who hold eternal conscious torment do not hold that. Some argue that the misery
of the lost is real but bounded in intensity, or that it varies by degree, or
that the two destinies are not commensurable quantities at all and cannot be
placed on a single scale. Anyone who rejects the symmetry premise is not
touched by this branch of the argument. The section on why hell is not the
opposite of heaven takes that question up directly.
</Callout>

## Case two: the assumptions of conditional immortality

On the conditionalist view the consequence of hell is finite, and the greatness
of heaven far surpasses it.

<Scripture reference="Romans 5:15-18" />

So the value of heaven is unbounded and positive, while the value of hell,
however severe, is a finite negative quantity. The negative of the value of
hell is therefore much smaller than the value of heaven, and this remains true
even after both are multiplied by their head counts, because a finite number
multiplied by a finite value stays finite while the other side does not.

The hell term is therefore negligible against the heaven term, and the equation
reduces to:

Number in heaven × Value of heaven, approximately equals the net outcome of
humanity

On this branch the net value of the end state of humanity is positive without
bound.

## The assumptions, stated honestly

The two branches give opposite answers. It would be easy to present that as a
result. It is not one, and the reasons are worth setting out plainly.

- **The arithmetic on infinite quantities is informal.** Multiplying a finite
  head count by an infinite value, and subtracting one infinite quantity from
  another, are not well-defined operations in standard mathematics. The step
  that gathers the two terms in case one, and the step that discards the hell
  term in case two, both treat infinity as though it behaved like a large
  number. It does not. These are illustrations of a shape, not proofs.
- **It assumes experiences can be summed across persons.** The equation
  supposes that one person's joy and another person's misery are quantities of
  the same kind, which can be added and subtracted. That is a substantial
  philosophical commitment, and many would not grant it.
- **It assumes both head counts are finite.** Nothing establishes that, and the
  equation behaves very differently if either is not.
- **It assumes the values can be signed at all.** Calling the experience of the
  lost a negative number, and that of the saved a positive one, is a modelling
  choice, not something Scripture states.
- **A net total may not be the right question.** Even if every step worked, it
  is not clear that God's justice is the sort of thing that shows up in a
  total.

## The author's closing note

The author adds two qualifications of his own.

The first is that it is not all about humanity. The glory of God could be
factored in as a further term, though he does not think that term would differ
between the two views, so it would not change the comparison.

The second is that a more complicated formula could divide experience into a
quality part and a duration part, and could include earthly experience
alongside the final state. He expects the resulting equations would simplify
the same way, because infinity is involved either way.

## What this illustration establishes

- Under a symmetrical view of the two destinies, and given that the lost are
  the larger group, the summed outcome is dominated by that larger group.

### What it does not establish

- Anything about what Scripture teaches. The author says this himself, in
  capitals, at the head of the original.
- That arithmetic on infinite quantities of this kind is well defined, or that
  the steps above would survive a rigorous treatment.
- That defenders of the traditional view are committed to the symmetry premise
  the first branch requires. Many are not.
