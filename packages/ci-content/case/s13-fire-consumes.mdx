## In brief

Jesus ends the parable of the weeds with the weeds gathered, bound and burned. That
is what fire does throughout Scripture: it consumes what is put into it. Where
something in a fire is not consumed, the text says so explicitly, treats it as
remarkable, and credits the preservation to God. The distinction between the ordinary
case and the marked exception is the whole of this argument.

The argument does not claim that God is unable to sustain a person in fire. Scripture
records that he has done exactly that. It claims something narrower: when Scripture
wants a reader to understand that something in a fire survives, it says so plainly, and
the parables of judgment say the opposite.

Two positions are in view. Eternal conscious torment (ECT) holds that the unrighteous
suffer consciously and without end. Conditional immortality (CI) holds that immortality
is given in Christ, and that the unrighteous are finally destroyed after judgment.

## The primary passage

<Scripture reference="Matthew 13:36-43" />

The parable itself stands at Matthew 13:24-30. A man sows good seed, an enemy sows
weeds among it, and the owner lets both grow until the harvest, when the weeds are
bound in bundles and burned and the wheat is gathered into the barn. The interpretation
above is Jesus' own. The harvest is the end of the age, the reapers are angels, and
what happens to the weeds is what happens to those gathered out of the kingdom.

### The word rendered "burned"

The verb behind "burned" in verse 40 is the compound form *katakaio*, which ordinarily
carries the sense of burning something up rather than merely setting it alight. The source
document points to the same verb behind the burning in Revelation 8:7, Acts 19:19 and
Hebrews 13:11, and in each of those the thing burned is consumed and does not remain. The
claim is not that the fire is painless. It is that the fire has a finished result.

Malachi 4:1-3 gives the same picture in prophetic form.

<Scripture reference="Malachi 4:1-3" />

### What the illustration implies

Weeds in a fire burn up and are gone. Weeds are also among the easiest things to
destroy by fire, which makes them a strange image to choose if the intended meaning
were a body sustained indefinitely against the flame. Nothing in the behaviour of weeds
in a furnace suggests survival.

The furnace in the parable is burning away everything that is gathered out of the
kingdom. On the reading offered here, that is the whole of what is thrown in, since
Isaiah 64:6 denies that even the righteous deeds of the unrighteous are clean. If the
fire finishes its work, there is nothing left over to persist.

The obvious objection is verse 42: there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth. That
phrase is treated in full at
[Weeping, Gnashing of Teeth, and Outer Darkness](/case/key-texts/weeping-and-gnashing/),
and it is not answered here.

## Why ECT interpreters read the fire differently

Defenders of eternal conscious torment do not dispute that ordinary fire consumes.
Their point is that the fire of final judgment is not ordinary fire. It is the
instrument of God's own judgment, and its effect is whatever he determines it to be.
Scripture supplies the precedent directly: a bush burned and was not consumed, and
three men walked in a furnace so hot that it killed the men who threw them in. If God
can do that, the ordinary behaviour of fire settles nothing about what this fire does.
On this reading the imagery of harvest and furnace teaches the certainty, the finality
and the terror of judgment rather than the mechanics of it, and the weeping and
gnashing of teeth in verse 42 describes people who are still present to weep.

This is a serious argument and it should not be answered by denying what it affirms.
God can preserve a creature in fire. The response below turns on something else.

## The conditionalist response: marked and unmarked cases

Every biblical case of something in a fire that is not destroyed is flagged by the text
as out of the ordinary.

- **The burning bush.** Exodus 3:2 draws attention to the fact that the bush burned and
  was not consumed. The non-consumption is the sign. If bushes ordinarily survived fire
  there would have been nothing to see.
- **Isaiah 43:2.** What is given here is a promise that the one God is with will not be
  consumed when he walks through fire. It is something God undertakes for his people, not
  a description of how fire normally behaves.
- **Daniel 3.** The narrative dwells at length on the astonishment of the king and the
  detail that the men were untouched. Again, the preservation is the point of the story.
- **1 Corinthians 3:11-15.** Fire tests each builder's work. What survives, survives;
  what is burned up is lost. Notice that the fire here does consume, and the text has to
  state separately that the builder himself is saved.
- **Luke 16:24.** The rich man speaks of agony in fire. His brothers are still alive, so
  this is the intermediate state, Hades, and not the final judgment. Something is
  sustaining him there. That is treated at
  [Why Final Judgment Is Delayed](/case/biblical-language/delayed-judgment/) and
  [The Rich Man and Lazarus](/case/key-texts/rich-man-and-lazarus/).

<Scripture reference="Exodus 3:2" />

In each case the survival is stated, and stated as something worth stating. The parable
of the weeds contains no such marker. Neither do the parallel images of chaff, stubble
and dead branches. The ECT reading asks the unmarked case to behave like the marked
exceptions with nothing in the text to signal it, which reverses the way Scripture
itself flags the difference.

Keep the categories distinct while reading this. Hades is the intermediate state, the
Valley of Hinnom lies behind the word Gehenna, and the lake of fire belongs to the final
judgment scene in Revelation 20. They are not interchangeable, and an argument that
moves between them without saying so has proved less than it appears.

## Fire paired with sulphur

Where Scripture pairs fire with sulphur, the result is consistently the destruction of
what is burned rather than its preservation. The pairing appears at Genesis 19:24-27,
Deuteronomy 29:22-23, Isaiah 34:9-10, Ezekiel 38:22 and Revelation 9:17-18, and in every
one of those the outcome is a place or a people made desolate.

<Scripture reference="Deuteronomy 29:22-23" />

The same pairing appears in Revelation 14:10, 19:20, 20:10 and 21:8. Joseph Dear
discusses the pattern at length. <Cite id="dear-bible-teaches-annihilationism" locator="page 121" />

One qualification belongs here. Revelation 20:10 attaches torment day and night for ever
and ever to the devil, the beast and the false prophet. Whether that clause extends to
the human beings thrown in later is a separate question, argued at
[Revelation 20: The Lake of Fire and the Second Death](/case/key-texts/lake-of-fire/).
The sulphur pattern is evidence about what the image ordinarily signifies. It does not
settle that verse on its own.

## God himself as the consuming fire

<Scripture reference="Hebrews 12:26-29" />

The description of God as a consuming fire arrives at the end of a passage about the
removal of what can be shaken so that what cannot be shaken may remain. Read in that
setting, the consuming fire is not a place into which things are put and kept. It is God
acting to remove. When other passages say the wicked are consumed, the same sense
follows: they are removed, and they do not remain.

## What this argument establishes

- Consuming is the ordinary and unmarked function of fire throughout Scripture.
- Preservation in fire is always presented as an exception, and always attributed to
  God's action.
- Fire paired with sulphur consistently marks complete destruction.
- Jesus chose an image of consumption to describe the end of the age.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That God could not sustain a person in the lake of fire if he chose to. The argument
  concerns what Scripture marks, not what God is able to do.
- That the lake of fire is literal rather than symbolic. Revelation trades heavily in
  images. The observation here is about what the chosen image signifies, which holds
  either way.
- That the wicked feel nothing. The parable adds weeping and gnashing of teeth, and this
  site does not claim that judgment is painless or instant.
