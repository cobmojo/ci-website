## In brief

When God judges in the Old Testament narrative, the judgment takes a recognisable shape.
Something is removed completely. Nothing is left to regrow. The flood, Sodom, the
Passover night, the Red Sea, Korah, the fire that fell on the captains, the nations
devoted to destruction and the death penalty in the law all follow that shape. Prolonged
torture never appears as a form of judgment that God commands or performs.

This is a pattern argument and it is offered as one. It does not prove by itself what
the final judgment will be. It does show what the Old Testament reader was trained to
expect when God acts in judgment, and that expectation is the background against which
the New Testament writers speak. The two positions in view are eternal conscious torment
(ECT), that the unrighteous suffer consciously and without end, and conditional
immortality (CI), that immortality is given in Christ and that the unrighteous are
finally destroyed.

## The recurring shape

### The flood

<Scripture reference="Genesis 6:5-8" />

The account of the flood is long, and it contains no description of what those who
died experienced. Not a line. If the point of the narrative were the suffering of the
judged, that is a strange omission. The point the text presses is the completeness of
the removal, and the single household preserved through it.

### Sodom and Gomorrah

Treated in full at
[Sodom and Gomorrah as the Example](/case/biblical-patterns/sodom-and-gomorrah/), which
is the one case an apostle explicitly labels an example of what is coming for the
ungodly. The cities were turned to ash in a day and were never rebuilt.

### The Passover and the Red Sea

At the Passover, the households marked by the blood of the lamb were passed over and the
firstborn of Egypt died. The judgment fell as death, not as an affliction that continued.
The pattern repeats days later at the sea, where the pursuing army is drowned rather than
subjected to anything prolonged.

<Scripture reference="Exodus 12:29-30" />

The Passover is also the clearest Old Testament picture of substitution: a life given so
that another life is spared. That thread is picked up at
[What the Sacrificial System Says About Sin's Penalty](/case/biblical-patterns/sacrificial-system/)
and at
[What Jesus' Death Says About the Penalty for Sin](/case/biblical-patterns/jesus-death/).

### Korah, and the fire on the captains

<Scripture reference="Numbers 16:35" />

Fire came out from the Lord and consumed the two hundred and fifty men offering incense.
The earth closed over the rest. In 2 Kings 1:10 fire falls from heaven and consumes a
captain and his fifty. In both cases the verb of the judgment is consumption, and in
both cases the account ends there.

### The nations devoted to destruction

<Scripture reference="Deuteronomy 7:1-2" />

The instructions given to Israel about the nations of Canaan are among the hardest
passages in the Old Testament, and this page does not attempt to resolve the moral
questions they raise. The narrow observation relevant here is about form. The command is
always to destroy completely and to leave nothing. It is never to inflict prolonged
suffering. See also Deuteronomy 20:16-17, Numbers 21:2-3, Joshua 6:17-24, Joshua 8:22
and Ezekiel 12:20, where the cities are laid waste and the land becomes desolate.

### The penalty in the law

For the gravest offences under the law, including murder, blasphemy and adultery, the
penalty prescribed is death. It is not imprisonment, and it is not torture. Whatever
else the law's penalties were doing, they set death as the ceiling of what the gravest
sin deserved under that covenant.

## Gehenna and the Valley of Hinnom

The word Jesus uses that English Bibles often render "hell" is Gehenna, a transliteration
of the Greek form of the Hebrew name for the Valley of the Son of Hinnom. It is a real
place outside Jerusalem, and it carried associations long before Jesus used it.

The valley is where child sacrifice was practised under Ahaz and Manasseh, an
abomination the historical books record with horror. Jeremiah then announces that the
name will change.

<Scripture reference="Jeremiah 7:32-33" />

Topheth becomes the Valley of Slaughter. The image Jeremiah leaves is of burial ground
overflowing, of corpses left exposed with nothing to drive the birds away. That is the
freight the name carries: mass death, dishonoured bodies, judgment made visible. It is
not an image of the conscious suffering of the living.

The same image stands behind Isaiah 66:24, which Jesus quotes in Mark 9:48, where the
worm and the fire work on dead bodies. That passage is treated at
[Mark 9: Undying Worms and Unquenchable Fire](/case/key-texts/mark-9/).

<Callout tone="caution" title="A disputed tradition" as="h3">
It is very often said, in sermons and in study Bibles, that in Jesus' day the Valley of
Hinnom was a burning rubbish dump where refuse and the bodies of criminals were
continually consumed, and that this is what gave Gehenna its force. That claim is not
repeated on this page.

No source from the period is known to describe such a dump, whether Jewish, Roman or
Christian. The tradition is widely regarded as a much later inference read back into the
first century, and it cannot bear the weight that is routinely placed on it by writers on
both sides of this debate.

The associations that are well attested are the ones set out above: a real valley, child
sacrifice under Ahaz and Manasseh, Jeremiah's renaming of it as the Valley of Slaughter,
unburied corpses, and its use in the prophets as an image of judgment. Those are enough
to establish the point, and they have the advantage of being in the text.
</Callout>

Keep the terms distinct while reading. Sheol and Hades name the state of the dead before
judgment. Gehenna is the word Jesus uses for the place of final destruction. The lake of
fire belongs to the judgment scene of Revelation 20. Collapsing all four into the single
English word "hell" is what allows arguments to slide from one to another unnoticed.

## What this pattern establishes

- Divine judgment in the Old Testament repeatedly takes the form of complete removal,
  with no survivors and no regrowth.
- Prolonged torment is never commanded by God as a judicial penalty, and never described
  as one he administers.
- The gravest offences under the law carried a death penalty rather than a prolonged one.
- Gehenna, the word Jesus chose, carries associations of slaughter and unburied bodies
  rather than of conscious suffering.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That temporal judgments settle the form of the final judgment. This is a pattern, and
  a pattern is evidence rather than proof. An ECT reader can consistently hold that the
  final judgment differs in kind from every temporal one.
- That the judgments described were painless. Scripture does not present them that way,
  and this site does not claim that final judgment is painless or instant.
- That the moral difficulties of the conquest narratives are answered here. They are not
  the subject of this page.
