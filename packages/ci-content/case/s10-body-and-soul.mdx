## In brief

Matthew 10:28 is one of the few places where Jesus speaks directly about what happens to
the soul in Gehenna, and it is cited on both sides. The verse ties the fate of the body
and the fate of the soul together, sets both alongside what a human killer can do, and
then uses a stronger word for what God can do than the word for killing.

Jesus is not saying the outcome in Gehenna is milder than being killed. He is saying it
is worse. The question is what a worse-than-killing outcome looks like, when the word
chosen for it is one that elsewhere in these Gospels describes putting a person to death.

## Why traditional interpreters cite this passage

Defenders of eternal conscious torment (ECT) read the verse as a statement about
God’s authority over final destiny rather than about the cessation of the person. On
this reading, to destroy body and soul is to consign the whole person to a condition
of everlasting loss. The word can carry that sense: to destroy something is often to
ruin it, to render it fit for nothing, without ending its existence.

Read that way, the verse warns about who holds the final say. Human beings can end a
life; only God determines what becomes of a person for ever. The fear Jesus commands is
fear of the one whose verdict cannot be appealed. That is a coherent reading, not a
strained one.

## The argument step by step

### Jesus ties body and soul together

Whatever happens in Gehenna, Jesus says it happens to both. He does not describe a
body that perishes and a soul that survives, but puts them in one clause with one verb.

That has a consequence for the traditional reading. If the soul of the lost continues
consciously for ever, and the fate of the body is tied to the fate of the soul, then the
body must continue for ever as well. That raises the question of a resurrection to
imperishability for the unrighteous, taken up in
[The Biblical Case for Conditional Immortality](/case/final-destiny/conditional-immortality/).

### The comparison Jesus draws is with killing

The first half of the verse is about people who kill the body. Jesus does not say do not
fear those who can torture you. He says do not fear those who can kill you, and then turns
to the one who can do something greater. The frame of reference he supplies is the
ending of life.

### The word for what God does is the stronger one

Jesus uses one word for what people do to the body and a different word for what God can
do to body and soul. There are two possibilities.

The first is that the outcome in Gehenna is less severe than being killed. That seems
unlikely, because kill is already the strongest word available for what can be done to a
body on earth, and because Jesus is arguing that the second party is the one to fear.

The second is that the outcome is more severe, which fits the shape of the sentence
exactly. But if the word marks something more final than being killed, it is difficult
to make it mean something less final, which is what reading destroy as
ruin-while-continuing requires.

### How the verb is used elsewhere in the Synoptic Gospels

<Greek text="ἀπόλλυμι" transliteration="apollumi" gloss="destroy, kill, lose, ruin" />

This verb has a wide range: losing a coin, a wineskin being ruined, people perishing.
The observation below concerns one narrow slice.

Chris Date of Rethinking Hell points out that in Matthew, Mark and Luke there are nine
instances where this verb appears in the active voice and clearly describes one agent
acting on another. In the eight besides Matthew 10:28, it refers to literally killing a
person: Matthew 2:13, Matthew 12:14, Matthew 21:41, Matthew 22:7, Matthew 27:20, Mark
3:6, Mark 9:22 and Luke 6:9.
<Cite id="rethinking-hell" />

This is a pattern, not a rule. A word need not mean in one place what it means in eight
others, and no responsible argument claims it must. What the pattern does
is shift the question. If a writer wanted to say that God kills a person, this is a verb
he would naturally use, and in this construction these authors use it for nothing else.
Reading the ninth instance differently from the other eight is possible, but it is the
reading that needs the argument.

### Luke adds the order of events

Luke’s version says that the one to fear is the one who, after he has killed, has
authority to cast into Gehenna. The killing comes first and the casting follows. That
sequence matches the closing verse of Isaiah, quoted by Jesus in Mark 9, where the
objects of the worm and the fire are dead bodies.

<Scripture reference="Isaiah 66:24" />

The alignment is suggestive rather than conclusive, since Luke’s clause can be read as
describing the authority rather than a strict sequence. But on the more natural
reading, what is thrown into Gehenna has already been killed.

### The second death

<Scripture reference="Revelation 20:14" />

Something has to happen at that point for the phrase to mean anything. Unbelievers are
presently alive in the body and, on the standard account, spiritually dead in the sense
of being separated from God. If the state after the lake of fire is a continuing
conscious life, separated from God, in body and soul, it is difficult to identify what
died. That state is close to the intermediate state described in
[The Rich Man and Lazarus](/case/key-texts/rich-man-and-lazarus/), which raises the
question of what the resurrection and the judgment changed.

## What this argument establishes

- Jesus ties the fate of body and soul together rather than separating them.
- The comparison he draws is with the ending of life, not with continued suffering.
- The verb he chooses is one that elsewhere in these Gospels, in this construction,
  describes putting a person to death.
- Luke’s version places the killing before the casting into Gehenna.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That the soul is unconscious in the intermediate state.
- That the verb must always mean cessation. It has a wide range and often does not.
- Which anthropology is correct. Nothing in the argument above depends on choosing
  between the two set out below.

The author’s objections, in his own order of argument:

- **Option A.** No death would have occurred between the resurrected unbeliever and this
  state, since the spirit was already dead from birth. If nothing dies, the second death
  is not a death. It would also require the body to be raised imperishable, which he
  argues elsewhere is said of believers rather than of the unrighteous.
- **Option B.** The fates of body and soul are tied together in Matthew 10:28. If only
  the body dies, this is what is ordinarily called death, and the second death becomes
  the reversal of a resurrection that had just occurred.
- **Option C.** A body without a soul is not a person and cannot suffer consciously, and
  again the fates of body and soul are tied together.
