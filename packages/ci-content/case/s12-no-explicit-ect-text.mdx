## In brief

Scripture says a great deal about judgment, wrath, fire and the day of the Lord.
Given all of that, the source document makes a short observation: no
passage states, in so many words, that wicked human beings will be tormented
consciously and without end.

This is an argument from silence and is treated as one. It cannot carry the case by
itself, which is why it is labelled a supporting argument. The strongest reply is
stated below.

## The observation

The doctrine of eternal conscious torment (ECT) claims that finally condemned human
beings remain alive, aware and suffering, for ever. That is a claim about duration,
about consciousness, and about human beings.

The observation is that no verse puts those three elements together explicitly. The
texts that speak of unending duration in the clearest terms are few, and each has a
feature that must be argued about: what the phrase attaches to, or who is in view, or
whether the noun names an act or a result. Those arguments are made at length in the
key-text pages.

## The reply from defenders of eternal conscious torment

The reply is strong, in two parts.

The first is that the doctrine is stated plainly enough already. Revelation 14:11
speaks of the smoke of their torment going up for ever and of having no rest day or
night. Revelation 20:10 has the devil, the beast and the false prophet tormented day
and night for ever, and five verses later everyone whose name is not found in the book
of life is thrown into the same lake. Matthew 25:46 sets eternal punishment against
eternal life. To a reader who has not adopted another framework, those look like plain
statements.

The second part concerns the standard being applied. Requiring a doctrine to appear in
one prescribed form of words is a test almost nothing would pass. Scripture nowhere uses
the word Trinity, or states in one sentence that Christ has two natures in one person.
Doctrines are drawn from what Scripture says taken together, and conditionalism would be
applying a rule it does not apply to itself.

## What the reply gets right, and what remains

The reply has force, and this page concedes it. An argument from absence proves nothing
on its own, and the passages above are genuine evidence, treated seriously rather than
waved away:

- [Mark 9: Undying Worms and Unquenchable Fire](/case/key-texts/mark-9/)
- [Revelation 14: The Smoke of Their Torment](/case/key-texts/revelation-14/)
- [Revelation 20: The Lake of Fire and the Second Death](/case/key-texts/lake-of-fire/)
- [What Does “Eternal Punishment” Mean?](/case/key-texts/eternal-punishment/)

What survives the reply is not the bare absence but a comparison. The vocabulary of
destruction, perishing and death appears across both Testaments in dozens of places, in
poetry, prophecy, gospel and letter, and in most of them nobody disputes what the words
ordinarily mean. The texts that most clearly support unending conscious torment are a
handful, are concentrated in one highly symbolic book, and each turns on a contested
point of grammar or reference.

That is a comparison about weight and distribution rather than about silence. It does
not show that the traditional reading of those texts is wrong. It does suggest the
doctrine rests on a narrower textual base than its prominence in preaching would suggest.

## What this argument establishes

- The clearest texts cited for the traditional view are few, and each is disputed on
  textual grounds.
- The destruction vocabulary is far more widespread and, in most of its occurrences, far
  less contested.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That eternal conscious torment is false. An argument from absence cannot carry that
  weight, and this page does not ask it to.
- That the passages in Revelation and Matthew have been answered. That work is done on
  the pages linked above, and a reader unpersuaded by them should discount this page.
