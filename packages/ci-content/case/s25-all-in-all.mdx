## In brief

This site does not teach universal salvation. The author of the source document states
plainly that he believes universalism is wrong, for a number of reasons, and nothing on
this page should be read as a step toward it. Conditional immortality (CI) and universal
reconciliation are different positions with different conclusions, and the difference is
not a matter of emphasis.

With that said, there is a set of passages that speak of all things and every knee, and
those passages have to be read by everyone. The argument on this page has two parts.
First, each of them is bounded by its own context and none of them teaches that everyone
is finally saved. Second, once the first point is granted, the language of unity and of
God being all in all reads more comfortably in a final state where no one persists in
rebellion than in one where rebellion continues without end.

That second part is an inference about which picture the language fits more naturally. It
is labelled as an inference, and it is not offered as proof of anything.

Eternal conscious torment (ECT) is the view that the finally unrighteous exist for ever in
conscious suffering.

## What universal reconciliation claims

The universal reconciliation view holds that final punishment is remedial and temporary,
that all people eventually repent, and that all are finally saved. It rests a great deal
of weight on a handful of texts about all, and it has to account for the many passages
that describe the fate of the unrighteous in terms of destruction, death and exclusion.
The source document's judgment is that it does not account for them, and this site follows
that judgment.

## The texts, one at a time

### God all in all

<Scripture reference="1 Corinthians 15:20-28" />

Verse 28 is the summit of the passage: the Son is made subject to the Father, so that God
may be all in all. The context is the sequence of the end. Christ reigns until every enemy
is put under his feet, and the last enemy destroyed is death.

The traditional reading is that all in all describes the uncontested and universally
acknowledged reign of God, in which no rival power operates and everything that exists is
finally ordered to him. On that reading, subdued enemies who continue to exist under
judgment are no obstacle at all: they are part of what has been put in order.

That reading is coherent. The observation on the other side is a question about how
naturally the phrase reads. If people remain who have wholly and permanently rejected
God, in what sense is God all in them? The phrase is not obviously the phrase one would
choose for a state containing a permanent population in permanent opposition.

### Bringing all things together under Christ

<Scripture reference="Ephesians 1:10" />

Paul describes God's purpose to bring unity to all things in heaven and on earth under
Christ. Again the traditional reading is available and reasonable: to sum up all things
under Christ is to bring everything under his headship, which includes what is subdued as
well as what is redeemed.

And again the observation is about fit. A permanent condition of conscious agony, ruin and
rejection of God is a strange thing to include under the heading of unity. It would be, on
the face of it, the clearest instance of disunity there could be.

### One righteous act, justification for all

<Scripture reference="Romans 5:18" />

Read on its own, the verse could be made to say that everyone is justified. Read in
context it cannot. Romans 5 is describing two representative figures and the two humanities
that come from them, and Paul has already said in verse 17 that those who reign in life are
those who receive the gift. The all who are justified are all who are in Christ.

The conditionalist observation here is a small one and it is worth stating carefully. If
the finally unrighteous cease to exist, then at the end of the story all who are saved and
all people are the same set, because there is nobody else. The verse remains true in the
bounded sense it has in Romans 5, and it also becomes true without qualification once the
final state has arrived. On the traditional view it remains true only in the bounded sense.

### Mercy on all

<Scripture reference="Romans 11:32" />

The context of Romans 11 is Paul's argument about Jews and Gentiles, and the all on whom
God has mercy is not every individual without exception. It is those who believe, drawn
from both groups. The same bounded reading applies, and the same observation about the
final state applies with it.

### Every knee, every tongue

The next two passages use every rather than all, and they are usually brought into the
same discussion.

<Scripture reference="Romans 14:10-11" />

Paul is speaking to believers throughout Romans 14, so it can be argued that the we who
stand before the judgment seat are believers. The source document notes that this does not
much matter for the point at hand, because Paul attaches the bowing and confessing to the
judgment seat. Whether the scene includes unbelievers or not, it is located at the
judgment, and both views of final punishment agree that unbelievers are consciously
present at the judgment. Bowing there is not evidence of eventual salvation.

<Scripture reference="Philippians 2:10-11" />

The same applies here, with one addition. The phrase in heaven and on earth and under the
earth describes the world in its present configuration. In Revelation 21 heaven and earth
are made new and come together, so a scene described in those terms is most likely a scene
before the final state rather than a description of it. On that reading the passage is not
depicting the lost bowing and confessing for all time. It probably describes what happens
at the final judgment. The word probably belongs there; this is a judgment about the most
natural setting of the phrase, not a demonstration.

## The inference

Here is the modest conclusion, stated as an inference rather than as a proof.

All six passages can be read consistently with either view of final punishment. None of
them teaches universalism, and none of them proves conditional immortality. But the
language they use has a shape, and the shape is completeness: all things united, every
knee bowed, God all in all. Language of that kind sits more easily in a final state where
the rebellion has ended than in one where it continues permanently in a sealed-off region
of the created order.

**That is an inference about fit, and inference about fit is the weakest kind of argument
in this debate.** It is included because the source document includes it, and because a
reader who has been persuaded by the exegetical arguments elsewhere on the site may find
that these passages read more smoothly than they did before. It should not persuade anyone
on its own, and it does not appear among the load-bearing arguments of this case.

## What this argument establishes

- The universal-sounding texts are bounded by their contexts and do not teach that
  everyone is finally saved.
- The scenes of universal acknowledgement in Romans 14 and Philippians 2 are located at
  the judgment rather than after it.
- A final state without persisting rebellion reads more naturally alongside the language
  of unity and of God being all in all.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That these texts are evidence for conditional immortality. They are not, and this page
  does not treat them as such.
- That the traditional reading of any of them fails. Each has a coherent traditional
  reading, given above.
- Anything at all in favour of universal reconciliation, which this site holds to be
  mistaken.
