## In brief

Scripture is consistent about what sin earns. The warning in the garden is death.
The sentence in Ezekiel is death. Paul’s one-line summary in Romans is death. James
traces the sequence from desire to sin to death. This page asks one narrow question
about all of it: which death is meant?

It cannot be only the death every human being already dies. Believers undergo that
death as well, and if physical death were the whole penalty, the unrighteous would
discharge it at the end of an ordinary life and the last judgment would have nothing
left to impose. The death held out as the wage of sin is the one Revelation names the
second death.

Conditional immortality (CI) holds that the finally unrighteous are destroyed rather
than kept alive in punishment. Eternal conscious torment (ECT) holds that they remain
alive and suffering without end. The dispute here is over what the word death is doing.

<Callout tone="author" title="From the author" as="h3">
Anyone who has lived inside one account of final punishment for long enough will
start reading every passage through it. The author’s own experience was that words
like perish, death and destruction had quietly come to carry a different sense: to
live for ever in torment, separated from God. His request is not that readers abandon
the traditional view, but that they notice when a word is being given a second sense
and ask whether Scripture requires that or whether it has been carried in from a
conclusion reached somewhere else. The caution runs both ways. A conditionalist can
just as easily hear cessation in a text that is making a different point.
</Callout>

## Why the death in view cannot be only physical death

Two observations do most of the work here, and both come straight from the texts.

The first is that believers die. If the wage of sin were the physical death that ends
a natural life, then those who are in Christ would not undergo it. They do. So either
the wage is unpaid in their case, which the passage does not allow, or physical death
is not the whole of what the word means.

The second is the reverse of the first. If physical death were the full penalty, the
unrighteous would pay it in full at the end of their own natural lives. Every person
who has ever died would have settled the account, and the resurrection of the
unrighteous to judgment would be raising people whose sentence had already been served.

Both observations point the same way. The death Scripture names as the wage of sin
lies beyond the death that everyone dies. Revelation gives it a name.

<Scripture reference="Revelation 20:14" />

## Why ECT interpreters read death as separation

Defenders of eternal conscious torment have a considered answer here, and it is not a
dodge. On the traditional reading, the death in these texts is spiritual death, which
means separation from God rather than the end of existence.

The reading has real support. Scripture does use death for a condition rather than an
event. The New Testament describes people who are walking around and breathing as dead
in their sins, and speaks of them being made alive in Christ. Adam and Eve did not fall
down lifeless on the day they ate, which on the traditional reading is exactly the
point: what died that day was the relationship. On this account the wage of sin is life
permanently severed from its source, a fate the tradition regards as worse, not lighter.

## The conditionalist response

Conditionalism does not deny that separation is part of what death means here. It
agrees that the estrangement is real, that it begins in this life, and that it is
what the final sentence confirms.

The response is that separation is where the argument starts rather than where it
ends. If a creature is cut off permanently from the one who is the source and
sustainer of its life, the question is what happens next. That question is taken up
in [Physical Death as a Picture of Final Death](/case/biblical-patterns/physical-and-final-death/),
which argues that separation from the giver of life does not settle into a stable
condition of endless living. It ends in the loss of life.

## Why the choice of the word death matters

The author closes this section with an observation about the image itself rather than
about any single verse.

Death is one of the very few things every human being understands without being
taught. No one needs Scripture to know what it looks like when a person dies: that
person no longer consciously exists here. This is knowledge available by general
revelation. When God chose a word to name the penalty for sin, that is the word he
used, from the first warning in Eden to the last chapters of Revelation.

If the intended meaning were unending conscious suffering, a good many other images
were available and none was used as the governing term. The point concerns the fitness
of the chosen image, which is a consideration rather than a proof. But it has to be
answered, because the traditional reading requires the plainest available word for the
end of life to carry a sense close to its opposite.

## What this argument establishes

- Death is the penalty Scripture names for sin, consistently, from Genesis through
  the Epistles.
- The death in view cannot be only physical death, since believers undergo that death
  and since it would otherwise be discharged before the judgment.
- The final penalty is identified in Revelation as the second death.
- The governing image for the penalty is the one every reader already understands
  without instruction.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That the death in view excludes conscious suffering along the way. Nothing here
  says the judgment is painless or instant.
- That separation is not part of what death means in these texts. Conditionalism
  affirms that it is.
- That the traditional reading has no answer. It has one, and the answer is engaged
  rather than dismissed above.
