## In brief

Scripture has a standard way of referring to the final reckoning. It calls it a day: the
day of judgment, the day of wrath, the day of the Lord. The question this page asks is
whether that day is presented as the occasion on which a sentence is passed and then runs
indefinitely afterwards, or as the occasion on which the sentence is carried out.

Several texts appear to put the judging and the destroying on the same day. One saying of
Jesus goes further and treats the bearability of the day itself as the thing that varies
from place to place, which is hard to account for if the day is only the announcement of
a far greater and unending punishment to follow.

The source document says this seems to imply a short, one-time event. That hedge is worth
keeping. This page does not claim to establish how long the day of judgment lasts.

Conditional immortality (CI) is the view that unending life is a gift given in Christ.
Eternal conscious torment (ECT) is the view that the finally unrighteous exist for ever in
conscious suffering.

## The texts

<Scripture reference="2 Peter 3:7-13" />

The clause that matters is in verse 7: the present heavens and earth are being kept for
the day of judgment and destruction of the ungodly. Judgment and destruction are named
together and attached to the same day. A few verses earlier Peter has used comparable
language about false teachers.

<Scripture reference="2 Peter 2:1" />

Their destruction is described as swift.

<Scripture reference="Zephaniah 1:15-18" />

Zephaniah piles up the word day and then describes what happens on it: distress,
devastation, blood poured out, the whole land consumed in the fire of God's jealousy, and
a full and sudden end made of the inhabitants of the earth. Whatever this passage's
primary historical reference, the day language in the prophets is the background against
which the New Testament writers speak, and in that background the day is when the thing
happens.

<Scripture reference="Romans 2:5" />

Paul's image is of wrath being stored up for a day. Storing something up for a day implies
that it is released on that day.

<Scripture reference="Matthew 10:15" />

This is the sharpest of the four. Jesus says the day of judgment will be more bearable for
Sodom and Gomorrah than for the town that rejects his messengers. The comparison is made
about the day, and about how bearable it is. If the day were the sentencing hearing before
an unending punishment, one would expect degrees of severity to be located in the
punishment rather than in the hearing.

Hebrews 9:27 fits the same shape, appointing death once and judgment after it.

The source document adds an analogy from the flood. On the day the flood came, everyone
not in the ark stopped living. It is an illustration of the pattern rather than an
argument from a text about final judgment.

## How ECT interpreters read the day

The traditional reading has good answers here, and they should be stated before they are
weighed.

First, day in biblical usage frequently denotes an epoch rather than a period of
twenty-four hours. The day of the Lord in the prophets covers extended campaigns of
judgment. Reading the day of judgment as a brief event imports a modern sense of the word
into texts that do not require it. Peter himself, in the passage quoted above, warns in
verse 8 that with the Lord a day is like a thousand years and a thousand years like a day.
That warning sits in the middle of the very chapter the conditionalist argument leans on.

Second, the courtroom pattern is entirely natural. A sentencing day is a single day, and
the sentence begins on it, and the sentence may run for a very long time afterwards. To
say that judgment and the destruction of the ungodly belong to the same day is not to say
that the destruction is complete within the day.

Third, on Matthew 10:15, degrees of bearability can be expressed within an unending state
as easily as within a single event. The traditional view has always held that there are
degrees of punishment, and Jesus' comparison can be read as a comparison of the sentences
handed down on that day rather than of what is experienced during it.

## The conditionalist response

The conditionalist reply does not claim that day must mean a short period. It claims that
several texts read more naturally if the sentence is executed rather than merely announced.

On 2 Peter 3:7, the phrase joins judgment and destruction to one day without any
indication that the second follows the first at a long interval. On Romans 2:5, wrath
stored up for the day of wrath is most naturally wrath discharged on that day. On
Matthew 10:15, the traditional reading is available, but it requires the day to stand in
for what is decided on it, whereas the sentence as it stands attaches bearability to the
day itself.

None of these is decisive on its own. Taken together they form a pattern, and the pattern
points toward the day being when the punishment happens.

## A theological reflection about when justice is complete

The source document sets out a comparison at this point between the two views, measured by
how much of the deserved justice has actually been administered at any given moment.

**This is a theological reflection rather than an argument from Scripture, and it is
presented here on those terms.** No verse says anything about percentages of justice
administered, and the framing is the source document's own way of making a point about the
shape of the two positions.

The comparison runs as follows. On the conditionalist view, the active punishing takes
place on the day of judgment, and the new creation begins with the penalty for sin fully
paid out. On the traditional view, punishment is administered continuously and without
end, so that at any moment one cares to name, whether the first day of eternity or ten
thousand years in, the punishment due for a given sin is still being paid and has not been
completed.

Defenders of the traditional view are not without a response, and the response is a strong
one. The idea of justice arriving in instalments assumes that the penalty is a fixed
quantity being drawn down, which is precisely what the traditional view denies. On the
infinite-offence argument, the penalty is not a finite sum that could ever be complete, so
asking what proportion of it has been paid is a category mistake. Others answer that God's
justice is fully displayed at every moment of an ongoing sentence, so nothing is deficient
at any point. The infinite-offence argument itself is examined in
[RB3](/case/roadblocks/infinite-punishment/).

The source document's own conclusion is more modest than the comparison suggests. Both
views should hold that God applies exactly the right measure of punishment. If that is so,
there is no reason in principle why God could not punish quickly and far more intensely
and bring the judgment to completion, so that the new creation described in Revelation 21
can begin. The only thing that rules this out is a prior commitment to punishment that
must be infinite in duration, which is the question RB3 takes up.

## What this argument establishes

- Scripture repeatedly locates judgment and the destruction of the ungodly on the same
  day.
- In Matthew 10:15 Jesus treats the day of judgment itself as the thing whose severity
  varies from place to place.
- The prophetic day-of-the-Lord language describes events carried out, not sentences
  merely announced.

### What it does not establish by itself

- The literal duration of the day of judgment. Day in Scripture can denote an epoch, and
  nothing here settles the question.
- That the conscious punishment on that day is brief or light. Conditionalism holds that
  the judgment is consciously experienced and severe.
- That the traditional reading is impossible. It accounts for the day language by a
  courtroom pattern that is familiar and coherent.

### An open question

2 Peter 3:8, in the middle of the chapter this argument uses, cautions against measuring
God's timescales by ours. That caution applies to conditionalists as much as to anyone
else. The argument here is about what happens on the day, not about how long the day is,
and readers who want a duration will not find one in these texts.
