## In brief

Would God bring to nothing the crown of his own creation? The objection says
no: human beings are made in God's image, they are the summit of what he made
and called very good, and for the Creator to erase one of them would be to
concede that sin had defeated his purpose. In the source document this
objection is attributed to Hank Hanegraaff.

This page argues that the premise is right and the conclusion does not follow.
Human beings really are the crown of creation and really do bear God's image.
What is not obvious is that sustaining a broken image-bearer for ever in ruin
honours the image more than removing what has been irreparably broken.

<Callout tone="note" title="This page reasons theologically" as="h3">
This is a theological inference, not an exegesis of a disputed passage. It
argues about what follows from a doctrine both sides hold, and it is filed on
this site under that heading. The weight of the biblical case is carried
elsewhere.
</Callout>

## The objection, stated properly

Genesis says God made humanity in his own image and, having made them, called
the whole work very good. Everything else in the creation account is made after
its kind; only the human creature is made after God. That singles out human
beings as bearers of something of God himself, which is why murder is treated
in Scripture as an assault on God's own likeness and why human life carries a
dignity no other creature has.

<Scripture reference="Genesis 1:26-27" />

From there the objection runs: to annihilate an image-bearer is not merely to
end a life. It is to destroy something that carries the divine likeness. A God
who did that would be undoing his own masterwork, and doing it at the
instigation of sin. Preserving the person, even in judgment, at least preserves
what God made. On this view eternal conscious torment (ECT) is the position
that takes the dignity of the human creature most seriously.

There is something instinctively right in that, and it should be said plainly:
mankind is the crowning jewel of God's creation.

## How high a view of the image this site actually holds

It is worth spelling out how much of the objection's premise is affirmed here,
because conditionalism is sometimes assumed to hold a low view of the human
creature. It does not.

<Scripture reference="1 Corinthians 11:7" />

Man was designed to be the image and glory of God. When Adam was made, he was
meant to be what the physical world could look at and say, that is what God
looks like.

<Scripture reference="Romans 3:23" />

Then sin came, and Adam fell short of being the glory of God. The image was not
erased, but the creature no longer showed what he was made to show.

<Scripture reference="2 Corinthians 4:4" />

Jesus came in the flesh as the sinless and perfect image of God, described in
Colossians 1:15 as the image of the invisible God. He did what Adam failed to
do, and he died for our sins in order to restore us.

<Scripture reference="2 Corinthians 3:18" />

That restoration is the point of the whole arrangement. Human beings truly are
the crowning jewel of God's creation, because they were made in his image, and
the gospel is the recovery of what that image was for.

## Why the conclusion does not follow

There is something strange in the shape of the objection. It proposes that God
would hesitate to bring a human being to an end, but would not hesitate to
sustain that same human being, in the ruined state, for ever. If the concern is
for the honour of the image, it is not clear that the second option protects it
better than the first. A likeness kept indefinitely in a condition of permanent
rebellion and permanent misery is not an obvious tribute to the one it
resembles.

The conditionalist claim is narrower than it may sound. It is not that human
beings are cheap or disposable. It is that when the image-bearer is broken, and
has finally rejected the one possibility of being made whole again, Scripture
says the outcome is destruction rather than preservation. There will be no
tainted images in the age to come. On this reading it is all or nothing, and
the reason is not indifference to the image but seriousness about it.

The second half of that claim is not an inference. It is what the texts say. A
long line of passages describes God destroying the wicked, perishing being the
alternative to eternal life, and the end of the unrighteous being death. That
material is set out in the section on destruction. What Scripture never says,
anywhere, is that God is unwilling or unable to bring the wicked to an end
because of the image they bear.

## What this argument establishes

- The premise of the objection is affirmed rather than denied. Human beings
  bear God's image and are the crown of his creation.
- No passage of Scripture states that God will not destroy those who finally
  reject him, and a great many state that he will.
- Preserving a ruined image-bearer for ever is not self-evidently a higher
  regard for the image than removing what cannot be repaired.

### What it does not establish by itself

This page does not offer a complete theology of the image of God, and it does
not settle what becomes of the image in the final state. It answers one
objection. Nothing here is offered as positive evidence for conditional
immortality; the argument that Scripture teaches the destruction of the wicked
is made in the sections on biblical language, not on this page.

The attribution of the objection to Hank Hanegraaff comes from the source
document. No specific published statement of his is quoted or cited here, and
readers who want his own wording should consult his own work rather than rely
on this summary.
