## In brief

Under the law, atonement was made by the death of the sacrifice. The animal was killed.
It was not kept alive in pain, and the length of its suffering is never the measure of
anything. The system says that what sin costs is a life.

This is a short supporting page. It does not by itself decide between eternal conscious
torment (ECT) and conditional immortality (CI), and both positions affirm everything in
the paragraph above. What it contributes is one more instance of a consistent shape: when
Scripture shows the penalty for sin rather than describing it in the abstract, the
penalty is death.

## What the sacrifices did

The offerings of the Old Testament were killed, and killed quickly. The ritual attends
carefully to how the animal is presented, where it is slaughtered, what is done with the
blood and what is burned. It attends not at all to how long the creature suffered,
because that was never the point. The death was the consequence that the worshipper's
sin had earned, borne by something else.

That is the substitution the whole system depends on. What the worshipper should have
received was received by the animal in his place, and what the animal received was death.

## Why the blood

<Scripture reference="Leviticus 17:11" />

The reason given is precise. The blood makes atonement because the life is in it. The
shedding of blood matters because it is the giving up of a life, and the life is what
the altar requires. Hebrews restates the principle in summary form.

<Scripture reference="Hebrews 9:22" />

This is worth stating plainly, because it settles what kind of thing the sacrificial
system treats sin as costing. Not a duration. Not an intensity. A life.

## What the system pointed to

<Scripture reference="Hebrews 10:1-14" />

Hebrews argues that the repetition of the sacrifices was itself the proof of their
insufficiency. A sacrifice that had actually finished the matter would not have needed
repeating. The whole arrangement was a shadow, pointing forward to the one offering that
does finish it.

The argument matters here because of what the shadow was a shadow of. If the penalty
pictured by the sacrifices is death, and if Christ is the reality those sacrifices
anticipated, then the penalty he bore is the one they pictured. That line of reasoning is
taken up at
[What Jesus' Death Says About the Penalty for Sin](/case/biblical-patterns/jesus-death/).

## What this establishes

- Atonement under the law was made by the death of the sacrifice.
- The blood made atonement because the life of the creature is carried in it.
- Sacrificial animals were killed rather than subjected to prolonged suffering, and the
  texts show no interest in how much they suffered.
- The system was provisional and pointed to Christ, who is presented as its fulfilment.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That the sacrifices picture every dimension of the final judgment. They are a shadow of
  one thing, the atoning work of Christ, and they were never designed as a map of
  eschatology.
- That suffering has no place in judgment. Nothing here says that. It says only that the
  sacrificial system located the cost of sin in a life given up.
