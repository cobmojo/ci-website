## In brief

This page exists because the passage is frequently cited, not because it advances the
positive case. The account says little in support of conditional immortality (CI),
the view that unending life is a gift given to the redeemed rather than a property
everyone already possesses. It also says nothing in support of eternal conscious
torment (ECT), the view that the lost suffer consciously without end, which is why it
is worth treating carefully.

The decisive observation is that the rich man's five brothers are still alive while
he is speaking, which places the scene before the resurrection and the last judgment.
The word for where he is, moreover, is Hades, not Gehenna.

## The primary passage

<Scripture reference="Luke 16:19-31" />

## Why ECT interpreters cite this passage

The passage is cited because of how direct it is. Jesus himself describes the
scene, and he describes a man who is conscious after death, who is in torment,
who speaks of agony in fire, who sees and remembers and reasons, and who is told
that a great gulf is fixed so that nobody can cross. Conscious suffering, fire,
and a fixed and irreversible state are the three things the traditional view most
wants to establish, and all three stand together here in the words of Jesus.

Defenders of the traditional view add that Jesus was under no obligation to
describe the afterlife this way. If the wicked simply pass out of existence at
death, this is an extraordinary picture to paint, and the details are not
incidental: the rich man's awareness of his condition drives the whole exchange.

## Why this describes the intermediate state

Conditionalism does not dispute that the scene is conscious, and it does not
dispute that it is grim. It observes what the passage indicates about when the
scene takes place.

The rich man asks that Lazarus be sent to his five brothers so that they will not
come to this place, and Abraham replies that they have Moses and the prophets.
That exchange is unintelligible if the resurrection and the last judgment have
already happened, because there would be no living brothers left on earth to warn.
The scene therefore sits before the last judgment, and so is not a description of
the final state.

The vocabulary points the same way. Some translations render Hades, Sheol and
Gehenna all as "hell", which flattens three distinct terms into one. The word here
is Hades, the place of the dead before judgment, not Gehenna, the term Jesus uses
in his warnings about final punishment. Revelation keeps them distinct too, and
describes Hades itself being emptied and thrown into the lake of fire:

<Scripture reference="Revelation 20:13-14" />

Hades is not the last word about anyone in it. Nothing in the account indicates
that the rich man was going to live for ever; that he continues in Hades at all is
what the section on delayed judgment (S09) addresses.

## Parable or report

Interpreters disagree about whether this is a parable using stock imagery or a report
about a real man. The question does not affect the argument. Either way the brothers
are alive, and either way the word is Hades.

## Two observations the author adds

The author suggests that the rich man's suffering is likely similar to the weeping
and gnashing of teeth described elsewhere, and that if a person can suffer this way
before judgment, they could suffer accordingly on the Day of Judgment too, between
being judged and being no more. Both are offered as likelihoods rather than
conclusions, and are treated in S06 and S23.

He then raises a question rather than an argument. If this is what the intermediate
state is like, the traditional account of the lake of fire appears to reproduce the
same condition without adding to it. Why raise and judge the wicked only to return
them to the state they were already in? That is a question about the coherence of the
traditional picture, not evidence about what Luke 16 teaches.

## What this argument establishes

- The scene takes place before the resurrection and the final judgment, because
  the rich man's brothers are still alive.
- Hades and Gehenna are distinct terms and are not interchangeable here.
- Conscious experience in the intermediate state is not evidence about the final
  state, in either direction.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That the passage supports conditional immortality. It is neutral between the
  two views.
- Whether the account is a parable or a report about a real person.
- What the intermediate state is like for anyone else, or whether it is uniform.

<Callout tone="note" title="Why this page is here" as="h3">
The passage appears in almost every popular treatment of the subject, and readers who
have met it there deserve an account of it. A conditionalist who never mentioned
Luke 16 would lose no ground, and a defender of the traditional view who rested a
case on it would be resting it on the intermediate state rather than on final
punishment.
</Callout>
