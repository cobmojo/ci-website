## In brief

In the judgment scene at the end of Matthew 25, two destinations are described as
prepared. The righteous inherit a kingdom prepared for them from the foundation of the
world. The eternal fire was prepared for the devil and his angels. Those are the only two
places the passage says were made ready in advance, and the unrighteous are the one group
with nothing prepared for them.

The suggestion is that this is what we should expect if the unrighteous do not continue
for ever. A permanent population needs a permanent home. A sentence that ends does not.

This is an argument from what a text does not say, and this page says so plainly. It
carries very little on its own. It is offered as one small piece of a cumulative case,
and it is worth only as much as the rest of that case is worth.

Conditional immortality (CI) is the view that endless life is a gift given in Christ and
not a property every human possesses. Eternal conscious torment (ECT) is the view that
the finally unrighteous exist for ever in conscious suffering.

## The passage

<Scripture reference="Matthew 25:31-46" />

## Three parties, two prepared places

Read the scene as a list of who receives what.

The righteous receive a kingdom, and Jesus says it was prepared for them from the
foundation of the world. The devil and his angels have an eternal fire, and Jesus says
that too was prepared, with them named as the ones it was prepared for. The unrighteous
people on the left are sent into that fire. Nothing in the passage describes a place made
ready for them.

The language of preparation is not incidental to Matthew's Gospel or to the New
Testament. Jesus uses it again in John 14:2-3, where he goes to prepare a place and
promises to return so that his people may be where he is. Preparation implies permanence,
a dwelling made ready because someone will be living in it.

## How ECT interpreters read this

The traditional reading has a straightforward answer, and it is a good one.

The eternal fire is prepared. The unrighteous are sent into it. A place does not need to
be prepared separately for every group that ends up there, and the passage says nothing
about the unrighteous having no destination; it says exactly where they go. On this
reading, the asymmetry in the wording is explained by the point Jesus is making. The
kingdom was always intended for God's people. The fire was not made for human beings at
all, which is precisely the tragedy of the scene: people who were made for the kingdom
end up in a place prepared for someone else.

Defenders of the traditional view would also note the obvious methodological problem.
Arguments from silence are the weakest kind of argument, and it is easy to generate them
by noticing that any given text does not mention something. The burden is on anyone who
wants the silence to mean something.

## The conditionalist reading

The conditionalist response does not dispute any of that. It accepts that the fire is the
stated destination and that silence proves nothing by itself. The observation is narrower:
if the finally unrighteous were going to exist for ever, they would be permanent
inhabitants of the created order in the age to come, and it is at least notable that the
one passage which itemises what has been prepared, and for whom, has nothing to itemise
for them. Whereas if their sentence terminates in death, there is nothing for them to
inhabit and so nothing to prepare.

That is an observation about fit, not a proof. It matters only if other arguments have
already made the conditionalist reading plausible.

### A related pattern in the parables

A similar shape appears elsewhere in Matthew's parables of the kingdom. In the parable of
the weeds and its explanation, the wheat is gathered into the barn and the weeds are
burned. In the parable of the net, the good fish are collected into containers and the
bad are thrown away.

<Scripture reference="Matthew 13:47-50" />

In each case the righteous have a receptacle and a destination that continues, and the
wicked have a process applied to them and no container at all. The parallel is suggestive
rather than probative. Parables are not built to answer questions of this kind, and no
weight should be placed on a detail of a parable that the parable itself does not draw
attention to. The images are noted here because the pattern is consistent, not because
any one of them settles anything.

## What this argument establishes

- Matthew 25 names a kingdom prepared for the righteous and an eternal fire prepared for
  the devil and his angels.
- No place is described in that passage as prepared for wicked human beings.
- The same pattern of a destination for the righteous and no comparable destination for
  the wicked recurs in the parables of the weeds and the net.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That the absence of a prepared place proves the unrighteous do not continue. Silence
  in a text is not a denial.
- That the traditional reading of Matthew 25 is unavailable. It reads the fire as the
  stated destination and accounts for the wording without difficulty.
- Anything about the duration or nature of the punishment described in verse 46, which is
  a separate question handled elsewhere.
