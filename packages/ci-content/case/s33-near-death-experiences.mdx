## In brief

People sometimes report vivid experiences during a medical crisis, and some of
those reports are of a place of judgment. They are often sincere, often
life-changing for the person who had them, and they are sometimes offered as
evidence for what final punishment is like.

The argument on this page is entirely methodological. Where a reported
experience conflicts with Scripture, Scripture governs. That principle is not
difficult, and it is not controversial among Christians. What matters is that
it be applied evenly, to reports of heaven and reports of hell alike.

## The objection, stated properly

The objection deserves better than a dismissal, because the person making it is
usually making a reasonable point.

Reports of this kind are not always vague. Some are detailed, some are given by
people with nothing to gain from telling them, and some come from people with
no prior religious commitment and no expectation of anything. If someone comes
back from the edge of death convinced they have seen a place of conscious
torment, it seems callous to shrug that off, and arbitrary to accept a person's
testimony about everything else in their life and refuse it here.

Behind the objection is a fair instinct: God has communicated through visions
before, and Scripture does not say that he stopped.

## The methodological reply

### Scripture governs where the two conflict

The Christian principle is not that experience is worthless. It is that
Scripture is the standard by which any claimed revelation is tested.

<Scripture reference="Galatians 1:8" />

<Scripture reference="Isaiah 8:20" />

Paul's test is not whether a message is delivered impressively, or whether the
messenger is sincere. It is whether it agrees with what has already been given.
That test applies to a vision reported in a hospital exactly as it applies to
anything else.

### The test has to run in both directions

Here is where the argument bites. When a Christian rejects a reported
experience of heaven, the ground for rejecting it is normally that the picture
of the afterlife it presents does not match what Scripture teaches. A report
implying that essentially everyone arrives safely, whatever they believed or
however they lived, conflicts directly with what Jesus says about the narrow
way.

<Scripture reference="Matthew 7:13-14" />

So: to the extent that reported experiences of heaven are set aside for
conflicting with Scripture, consistency requires the same treatment of reported
experiences of hell. A report cannot be admitted as doctrinal evidence when it
supports a conclusion we already hold and excluded when it does not. Either
reported experience is a source of doctrine or it is not.

The same holds for the content of the reports. Where an account of hell
reproduces the imagery of popular culture rather than the language of
Scripture, that account is measured against Scripture, not the other way round.
This is not a claim that the person is lying. It is a claim about what counts
as evidence for a doctrine.

The source document adds that such accounts frequently disagree with one
another over specific details. That is an observation about a body of material
this site has not surveyed, and it is reported here as the source's
observation rather than asserted. It is offered as part of the reason the
consistency question arises at all, not as a premise the argument rests on.

<Callout tone="caution" title="An empirical claim this site does not make" as="h3">
The source document asserts that most such experiences are positive and that
they typically portray an afterlife in which everyone or almost everyone is
saved. No evidence base is cited for that proportion: no dataset, no sampling
method, no definition of what counts as a report of this kind. This site
therefore does not repeat it as a quantitative claim.

The argument does not need it. The point is conditional, and it holds whatever
the true proportions turn out to be: whatever grounds we use to set aside a
reported experience of heaven, those same grounds must apply to a reported
experience of hell.
</Callout>

### Why new visions would be needed at all

There is also a question worth asking about purpose. If these are messages from
God, what are they for? Does he need to convey some new truth in order to
convince people to avoid judgment?

<Scripture reference="Luke 16:27-31" />

Abraham's answer to the rich man is that people who will not listen to Moses
and the Prophets will not be convinced by someone rising from the dead. That
answer was given about a body of revelation smaller than the one we now have.
Someone did rise from the dead, and it did not persuade those who had already
decided. The New Testament's position is that what has been given is
sufficient.

### No report can establish a duration

One further point is simple and often overlooked. Whatever these experiences
are, they end. The person returns and describes them. No reported experience
lasts for eternity, which means no reported experience could establish the
duration of anything.

That matters, because duration is the entire question in dispute between
conditional immortality (CI) and eternal conscious torment (ECT). Both sides
affirm that the judgment is real and that it is consciously experienced. A
report of a distressing experience of judgment, even taken entirely at face
value, would not tell us whether what was glimpsed continues without end or
comes to an end. It is not evidence on the point at issue.

## What this argument establishes

- A consistent method requires treating reported experiences of heaven and of
  hell in the same way.
- Where a reported experience conflicts with Scripture, Scripture governs.
- No such report could establish the duration of final punishment, which is the
  question actually in dispute.

### What it does not establish by itself

It does not establish any generalisation about what proportion of these reports
are of one kind or another. It does not establish that they do not occur, that
the people reporting them are mistaken about having experienced something, or
that there is no medical, psychological or other explanation for them. Those
are separate questions, and this page takes no position on them.
