## In brief

Peter tells his readers that God made Sodom and Gomorrah an example of what is coming
for the ungodly. He then names two things about the cities. They were turned to ashes,
and they were condemned to extinction. Neither of those belongs to a picture of
unending conscious existence in torment.

The argument of this page is about selection. Peter had a great deal he could have
drawn from the account in Genesis. What he chose tells us what he took the example to
be. The eternal conscious torment (ECT) reading of the verse is stated below in full
before it is answered, because a good deal turns on whether the example is the visible
event or something invisible that followed it.

## The primary passage

<Scripture reference="2 Peter 2:6" />

Jude describes the same event.

## What Peter selects

Two features, and only two.

1. **Turned to ashes.** The cities were reduced. What was there ceased to be there.
2. **Condemned to extinction.** The word is not one of confinement or of ongoing
   suffering. It names an overthrow, a ruin, an end.

The second phrase is worth a moment. The English Standard Version, which the source
document follows here, renders it "condemned them to extinction". Other translations,
including the World English Bible displayed above, render the same word as destruction
or ruin. The senses are close, and none of them describes a state that continues. The
argument here does not depend on the sharpest of the available renderings.

On the ECT view neither of these ever describes the ungodly. They are not reduced to
ashes and they do not become extinct. Whatever else the traditional reading says about
the passage, it has to say that the two things Peter singles out are not, in the end,
what happens.

## What Peter could have selected and did not

Genesis supplies material that would have served an ECT reading directly.

**Fire and sulphur.** God destroyed the cities with fire and sulphur. Interpreters
holding ECT frequently read the fire of Sodom as a foreshadowing of the eternal fire of
the lake of fire, and the connection is not strained. Yet Peter does not mention the
fire at all. Continuous burning is the central feature of the traditional picture of
hell, and it is absent from the one verse that calls Sodom an example. There is a
further oddity: the fire at Sodom destroyed what it burned, which is precisely what the
eternal fire of the traditional reading is said not to do.

**Torment.** There was certainly suffering. Fire and sulphur falling on a city inflicts
severe pain, and this site does not pretend otherwise. But the suffering of the
inhabitants is never the focus of any passage that mentions Sodom, in either Testament.
The focus is always the completeness of the destruction. Suffering is the central
feature of ECT, and Peter does not raise it.

## What should have been visible if this were a picture of unending torment

Two further things are missing.

**A ruined state that persists.** ECT describes people reduced to ruin who nevertheless
continue as persons capable of experiencing pain. If Sodom were the example of that,
the cities should have been left half destroyed and somehow surviving. They were not.
The destruction was complete and it was finished within a single day.

**Ongoing fire.** Something like repeated rebuilding met by repeated burning would fit.
Instead, when Abraham looked toward the plain the next morning, what he saw was smoke.
The fire had finished its work. Genesis 19:27-28 records the morning after, not a fire
still burning. And the cities were destroyed so thoroughly that they were never rebuilt.

<Scripture reference="Genesis 19:24-28" />

Taken together, the two things Peter names, the two he passes over, and the two that are
missing from the history all point the same way. As an example of what awaits the ungodly,
Sodom fits a final and complete destruction closely, and fits unending conscious torment
poorly.

## The ECT reading of "the example"

The strongest reply runs as follows. Peter is not pointing at the rubble. He is pointing
at the people. The physical overthrow of the cities was a visible sign of an invisible
and far more serious judgment, and the example Peter holds up is the fate of the
inhabitants themselves, who passed at death into a conscious punishment that continues.
Jude supports the reading by calling the fire eternal, which cannot be a description of
a fire that went out in a morning and must therefore reach beyond the historical event.
On this account the ashes are the sign and the endless punishment is the thing signified,
and Peter's readers, who knew Genesis well, would have understood him that way.

## Four difficulties with that reading

**First, it sets aside the physical event entirely.** The two features Peter actually
names, ashes and extinction, both belong to the cities. If the example is something
other than what happened to the cities, the terms of the verse do no work.

**Second, the example cannot be what the inhabitants will experience after the last
judgment.** That judgment has not happened. Peter and Jude both speak of Sodom in the
past tense, as something God did and set forth. Further, on the ECT view nobody is
turned to ashes or made extinct after the last judgment, so the description would not
fit even then. And Jesus says that it will be more bearable on the day of judgment for
the land of Sodom than for the town that rejects his messengers, which places the
judgment of Sodom's inhabitants still in the future and admits of degrees.

<Scripture reference="Matthew 10:15" />

**Third, the example is unlikely to be what the inhabitants are experiencing now.**
Suppose the reference is to their present condition in Hades, the intermediate state
described in the account of the rich man. Three problems follow. That condition is
entirely unobservable, which makes it a poor example to hold up to anyone living. It
would also be exactly the same condition as every other unrighteous person who has ever
died, which leaves no reason to single out these two cities; what singled them out was
the scale of the visible judgment. And the intermediate state does not resemble what
happened at Sodom in any of the respects set out above.

**Fourth, Ezekiel points the reader back to the visible event.** Ezekiel names the sin
of Sodom and then says that God did away with them.

<Scripture reference="Ezekiel 16:49-50" />

<Callout tone="caution" title="A wording that carries less weight than it appears" as="h3">
The source document leans on the New International Version, which ends verse 50 with
"as you have seen", and takes that phrase to prove the observable judgment is what is in
view. Other translations understand the same Hebrew clause to refer to God's own seeing
rather than the reader's, and render it accordingly. The larger point survives either
way, since the sin and the removal Ezekiel describes are the historical events. The
argument should not be rested on those four words alone.
</Callout>

Elsewhere the pattern is consistent. Sodom and Gomorrah appear as a byword for
desolation and overthrow, not for ongoing suffering: Deuteronomy 29:23, Isaiah 13:19-22,
Jeremiah 49:18, Lamentations 4:6 and Zephaniah 2:9. Jesus himself sets the flood and
Sodom side by side as pictures of the destruction that comes on the day he is revealed,
at Luke 17:26-30.

## Eternal fire in Jude 7

Jude's phrase deserves a direct answer rather than avoidance. He sets the cities out as
an example, and calls the fire they underwent eternal. The fire that fell on Sodom
did not continue burning; Abraham saw smoke the following morning. So "eternal" in Jude
cannot mean that the flames are still alight over the Dead Sea. What it can mean, and
what the history bears out, is that the effect of that fire is permanent. Ordinary fires
destroy cities and the cities are rebuilt. These were not, and have not been. On this
reading the adjective describes the permanence of the result rather than the duration of
the burning, which is the same sense discussed at
[What Does "Eternal Punishment" Mean?](/case/key-texts/eternal-punishment/).

## What this argument establishes

- Peter selects being turned to ashes and being condemned to extinction as the example
  of what is coming for the ungodly.
- Neither torment nor continuing fire is mentioned in any passage that treats Sodom as
  a pattern of judgment.
- The destruction was complete, finished quickly, and never reversed.
- Eternal fire in Jude describes a permanent effect, not a fire of unending duration.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That the inhabitants of Sodom suffered nothing. They plainly did, and Scripture does
  not treat their suffering as trivial. It simply does not make it the point.
- What the inhabitants of Sodom are experiencing now, in advance of the final judgment.
  Matthew 10:15 shows that judgment still lies ahead of them.
- That every detail of a temporal judgment maps onto the final one. The example is a
  pattern offered by an apostle, not a diagram.
