## In brief

There is a question worth putting to any account of final punishment: what actually
settles the account?

If the answer is suffering, then suffering is a currency, and currencies add up. A long
enough punishment would eventually amount to the full price, the debt would be discharged,
and release would follow. That road does not end at eternal conscious torment (ECT), the
view that the finally unrighteous exist for ever in conscious suffering. It ends at
universal reconciliation, which ECT and conditional immortality (CI) both reject.

Scripture names something else as the wage of sin. This page reasons about a position's
internal consistency rather than exegeting a passage.

## The wage is death

<Scripture reference="Romans 6:23" />

The penalty named is death, and the gift set against it is eternal life in Christ. The
same pattern runs through the sacrificial system, where atonement is made by a life given
up rather than by a period of pain endured, and Hebrews 9:22 ties forgiveness to blood
shed rather than to suffering undergone. Those arguments are developed in
[S07, on the wages of sin](/case/biblical-language/sin-and-death/),
[S16, on the sacrificial system](/case/biblical-patterns/sacrificial-system/) and
[S17, on Jesus' death](/case/biblical-patterns/jesus-death/).

If death is the penalty, suffering is part of how the penalty is administered rather than
the thing that discharges it, and the question of when it has added up to enough never
arises.

## The parable of the unforgiving servant

<Scripture reference="Matthew 18:23-35" />

The parable enters this discussion because it ends with the servant handed over to the
jailers until he should pay everything he owed. The source document makes three
observations.

Nothing suggests the king does something to make the man live for ever. Nothing suggests
he could pay the debt from inside the prison, where he has no means of earning anything.
And nothing suggests the suffering there is itself paying the debt down. The
straightforward implication is that the man dies in prison with the debt unpaid.

## How ECT interpreters answer

The traditional view has a direct answer, and it does not depend on treating suffering as
currency.

Defenders of ECT typically hold that the debt is unpayable in principle. On the
infinite-offence argument, sin against a God of infinite worth incurs a penalty no finite
creature can discharge, so until he should pay everything is a way of saying never. On
that reading the parable's ending supports unending imprisonment rather than death in
prison, and the objection misses its target, because nobody on that side claims the
suffering accumulates toward a total.

That answer is coherent, and this page does not defeat it. What it does is locate the real
disagreement. The traditional position here rests on the infinite-offence argument, a piece
of theological reasoning rather than a statement of Scripture, examined on its own terms in
[RB3](/case/roadblocks/infinite-punishment/).

The narrower point stands for anyone who does not take that route. To say both that the
wicked are paying for their sins by suffering and that they never finish requires care,
because payment language implies a sum and a sum implies an end.

## What this argument establishes

- Treating suffering as the payment for sin implies that the debt could eventually be
  discharged, which leads toward universal reconciliation rather than toward ECT.
- Scripture names death rather than a duration of suffering as the wage of sin.
- The parable gives no indication that the debt is being paid down in prison.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That suffering has no place in final judgment. Conditionalism holds that it does, and
  that the judgment is consciously experienced and severe.
- That the traditional view is committed to suffering as payment. Its main defenders are
  not, and their position is treated in RB3.
- Anything about final punishment directly from the parable, which is about forgiveness
  between people and ends in an image rather than a doctrine.
