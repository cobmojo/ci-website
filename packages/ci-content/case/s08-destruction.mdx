## In brief

This page is not built on one decisive verse. It is built on the weight of a
vocabulary. When Scripture describes what finally becomes of the wicked, it reaches
again and again for the same small set of words: they perish, they are consumed, they
are burned up, they vanish, they are cut off, they are destroyed. The words appear in
the Psalms, in the prophets, in the preaching of John the Baptist, in Paul, in James
and in Peter. They are not confined to one genre or one author, and they run in one
direction.

Defenders of eternal conscious torment (ECT) have a serious answer to this, which is
that to destroy something can mean to ruin it rather than to end it. That answer is
stated below in full before it is weighed. The conditionalist reply is that several of
these texts add language of completeness that ruin does not account for, and that one
of them draws the comparison with animals.

## The vocabulary, grouped by the kind of language it uses

### Perishing, vanishing and being cut off

The Psalms describe the end of the wicked as a disappearance. The comparison is with
things that do not persist: smoke, pasture grass at the end of summer, a memory that
is erased.

<Scripture reference="Psalms 37:20" />

Psalms 34:16 and 34:21 speak of the memory of evildoers being cut off from the earth
and of affliction slaying the wicked. Psalms 37:38 says sinners are destroyed together
and that there is no future for the wicked. Psalms 145:20 sets the Lord preserving all
who love him against the wicked, whom he destroys. Psalms 59:13 is the most explicit
of the group: the psalmist asks God to consume them until they are no more.

The vocabulary in this group is the vocabulary of absence. It describes something that
was there and then is not.

### Burning up and being consumed

The prophets use fire, and in every case the point of the fire is what it leaves
behind, which is nothing worth naming. Isaiah 1:31 has the strong man become tinder
and his work the spark that lights him, with nobody to put the fire out. Isaiah 33:12
compares the peoples to lime burned in a kiln and to cut thorns thrown on a fire.

Malachi supplies the fullest statement, and it is worth reading whole because of what
it adds.

<Scripture reference="Malachi 4:1-3" />

Nahum describes a complete end, twice, and adds that the trouble will not rise a
second time.

<Scripture reference="Nahum 1:6-10" />

Obadiah 15-18 says of the nations that they will be as though they had never been, and
that there will be no survivor for the house of Esau.

John the Baptist preaches in the same register.

<Scripture reference="Luke 3:9" />

Eight verses later he adds the picture of the threshing floor: the wheat is gathered
into the barn and the chaff is burned with unquenchable fire. Neither a felled tree
nor a heap of chaff is a promising image for something that endures a fire
indefinitely. Both are images of fuel.

### The plain noun in the letters

In the New Testament letters the word does not need a picture attached. It is used as
the name of an outcome.

<Scripture reference="Romans 9:22" />

<Scripture reference="Philippians 3:18-19" />

Philippians 1:28 tells a persecuted church that the courage of the believers is itself
a sign, of destruction for their opponents and of salvation for them. 1 Thessalonians
5:3 says destruction comes suddenly on those who are saying peace and safety, and that
they will not escape. James 4:12 identifies the one lawgiver and judge as the one who
is able to save and to destroy, setting the two verbs against each other as the two
possible outcomes.

Peter uses the same noun for the appointed day itself.

<Scripture reference="2 Peter 3:7-13" />

Peter has just described the flood in the preceding verse. The comparison he is
drawing is between the world that was destroyed by water and the world reserved for
fire. Whatever the people who died in the flood are now, nobody reading that account
pictures undying conscious torment in the water.

### The comparison Peter draws with animals

One text supplies a comparison that is difficult to read any other way.

<Scripture reference="2 Peter 2:12" />

Peter compares the people he is describing to irrational animals, creatures born to be
caught and destroyed, and says they will be destroyed in the same destruction. The
same word group does the work in both halves of the comparison. Nobody holds that
animals caught and killed are then tormented consciously for ever. The comparison is
chosen for what it conveys, and what it conveys is being killed.

## Why ECT interpreters read this as ruin rather than cessation

The traditional answer to all of this is a linguistic one, and it is a fair point about
how the words actually work.

To destroy a thing does not always mean to make it stop existing. A wrecked building
is destroyed, and it still stands. A ruined harvest is destroyed, and the grain is
still in the field. A destroyed marriage still has two people in it. In ordinary usage
the word marks the loss of what something was for rather than the loss of its
substance. On that reading, when Scripture says the wicked are destroyed, it says they
are ruined: stripped of everything that made human life good, cut off from God, put
beyond repair. That is a fate the tradition regards as more terrible than ceasing, not
less, and it is a genuine sense of the word rather than a strained one.

Some defenders press the point further and describe the lost as being in a permanent
condition of ruin, in which the destruction is an unending state rather than a
completed act.

## Weighing the reply

Ruin is a legitimate sense of this word group, and conditionalism should not pretend
otherwise. A single occurrence of destroy proves nothing on its own. The reply has to
be made from the texts that say more than destroy.

**Malachi rules out a remainder.** Not a root and not a branch will be left. The
image is chosen precisely to exclude a surviving part. A ruined building has walls; a
tree with neither root nor branch has nothing.

**Psalms 59 states the endpoint.** Consume them until they are no more. Being no more
is not a description of a ruined thing that persists.

**Obadiah states it as counterfactual.** As though they had never been. Ruin leaves
something that visibly was. Obadiah’s image removes even that.

**Malachi describes ashes underfoot.** Ash is the residue of a fire that has finished,
not the condition of something currently burning. The author adds an observation here
which is worth taking as an observation rather than a proof: if the wicked are
consciously present in the lake of fire for ever, then the righteous who trample the
ashes would have to be doing so inside it. Read literally, the picture makes better
sense if the burning has completed and what remains is residue.

**Peter’s comparison is with animals.** This is the hardest of the four for the ruin
reading, because it is not a metaphor about buildings or harvests. It is an explicit
comparison of one fate with another, using the same word for both.

There is also a difficulty internal to the stronger version of the traditional reply.
If the wicked are perpetually in the process of being destroyed and never actually
destroyed, then the word is describing something that never happens. A destruction
that is never completed is not what the word names anywhere else.

<Callout tone="caution" title="What the ashes do not require" as="h3">
That ashes remain does not mean every particle of a person must be removed from the
universe. The claim conditionalism is making concerns conscious existence, not matter.
God does not need to eliminate every molecule of a person for that person to stop
consciously existing, and no argument on this page depends on his doing so.
</Callout>

## What this argument establishes

- The dominant biblical vocabulary for the end of the wicked is the vocabulary of
  destruction, perishing, being consumed and being cut off.
- Several of these texts add explicit language of completeness that the ruin reading
  does not account for: no root or branch, consumed until they are no more, as though
  they had never been, ashes underfoot.
- The same word for destroy is applied to the wicked and to irrational animals in a
  deliberate comparison.
- A destruction that is never completed is not a use of the word found elsewhere.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That every occurrence of destroy in Scripture must mean cessation of existence. It
  does not, and the ruin sense is real.
- That no conscious suffering precedes the destruction. Nothing here makes the
  judgment painless or instantaneous.
- That the case is settled without the passages treated in the key-text pages. This is
  cumulative evidence, and cumulative evidence is answerable text by text.
