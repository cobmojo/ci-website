## In brief

Jesus closes his warning about Gehenna in Mark 9 by quoting the last verse of
Isaiah. In Isaiah the worm and the fire are at work on dead bodies. Eternal
conscious torment (ECT), the view that the lost suffer consciously without end,
reads the undying worm and the unquenchable fire as images of a punishment that
never stops. Conditional immortality (CI), the view that unending life is a gift
given to the redeemed rather than a property every human being already
possesses, reads the same images the way Isaiah uses them: as a picture of
destruction that nobody interrupts and nothing reverses.

The question is not whether the imagery is severe. It is severe on any reading.
The question is what the words themselves carry, and in particular what
"unquenchable" means everywhere else in Scripture.

## Why ECT interpreters cite this passage

The traditional reading of Mark 9 is not a stretch, and it deserves to be stated
in its strongest form before it is answered.

First, Jesus chose the imagery. He is not quoting an opponent or reporting a
common saying. He selects the image, applies it directly to Gehenna, and repeats
his warning with mounting seriousness: better to lose a hand, a foot, an eye,
than to be thrown in whole. Whatever Gehenna is, Jesus places it beyond every
loss a person can suffer in this life. A fate that is worse than any mutilation
is a fate worth describing in the most absolute terms available.

Second, both halves of the image are stated as negatives without limit. The worm
does not die. The fire is not quenched. Jesus does not say the worm finishes and
then dies, or that the fire burns down once it has consumed. The natural force of
a negative like that, on this reading, is a process with no terminus.

Third, and most weightily, an undying worm is a strange image if the point is
simple destruction. A worm that eats a corpse eventually runs out of corpse and
dies. If Jesus wanted a picture of a body wholly consumed, a worm that completes
its work and stops would say it. A worm that never dies suggests something it
never finishes. The same is argued for the fire: an unquenchable fire is always
burning, and a fire always burning implies fuel always there.

Fourth, Daniel 12:2 stands alongside Mark 9 in most traditional treatments, and it
is about resurrection. People who sleep in the dust awake, some to everlasting life
and some to shame and everlasting contempt. If the second group is simply
extinguished, raising them at all invites the question of why they were raised.

Fifth, this is not an eccentric reading imposed later. Widely used modern
translations render Mark 9 with wording like "the fire that never goes out" (NIV),
which reflects a long-standing understanding of the phrase rather than a novelty.

## The conditionalist reading

### The objects in Isaiah are corpses

Mark 9:48 is a direct quotation of Isaiah 66:24, and in Isaiah the worm and the
fire are working on dead bodies. That is not an inference from context; it is
what the verse says. The people going out to look are looking at corpses.

Jesus pulls that whole scene in with a single line. A hearer who knew Isaiah
would supply the rest: the dead bodies, the completeness of the destruction, and
the loathing of those who look on. On this reading, being thrown into Gehenna
means becoming like the bodies in Isaiah 66, no longer alive, and being regarded
with contempt by those who see.

It is worth noticing what Jesus does not do. If he meant to redirect Isaiah's
picture of corpses toward a picture of the living in unending agony, that is a
substantial correction to make, and he makes none. He quotes the line and leaves
it.

### The worm and the fire are both doing the same job

In Isaiah the worm and the fire are two agents with one task: to consume what is
left. The worm feeds on dead flesh. The fire burns the bodies up. The word for
burning here describes the act of burning something up rather than an experience
of pain, which is the ordinary function of fire throughout Scripture.

Read that way, the negatives are the point rather than a problem. If the worms
died, some of the body would be left uneaten. If the fire were quenched, some of
the body would be left unburned. Saying that the worm does not die and the fire
is not quenched is a way of saying that neither agent fails to finish. The stress
falls on completion, not on duration.

### "Unquenchable" means it cannot be put out

This is the hinge of the argument, and it is a point about ordinary usage.

Plenty of fires go out without ever being quenched. They go out because they have
finished consuming their fuel. To quench a fire is to put it out from the
outside, before it has done its work. An unquenchable fire is therefore a fire
nobody can stop.

The illustration in the source document makes it plain. If a house fire is
unquenchable, that means the firefighters could not put it out before it
destroyed the entire house. It does not mean the house is still burning today.

The Hebrew prophets use the phrase exactly this way. Ezekiel describes a fire
that will consume every green tree and every dry tree, and says twice that it
will not be quenched:

<Scripture reference="Ezekiel 20:47-48" />

Isaiah says the strong will become tinder and burn with none to quench them,
which is a picture of being burned up rather than of being tortured:

<Scripture reference="Isaiah 1:31" />

Jeremiah uses the same construction of the Lord's wrath going out like fire:

<Scripture reference="Jeremiah 4:4" />

The same idiom appears again in two further prophets:

<Scripture reference="Jeremiah 17:27" />

In each case the fire is unquenchable, and in each case the thing burning is
destroyed. Not one of these passages describes a fire that keeps something in
existence.

### A note on translation

Some widely used modern translations render the phrase in Mark 9 as "the fire
that never goes out" rather than as unquenchable fire. Those are not the same
claim. "Unquenchable" says nobody can put the fire out. "Never goes out" says
the fire has no end, which is an additional statement about duration.

The point here is narrow and it is about method, not motive. A reader who builds
a case for unending torment on the phrase "never goes out" is building on an
interpretive rendering rather than on the wording of the underlying text, and the
argument then rests on the interpretation it was meant to support. Anyone using
Mark 9 in this debate should work from the more literal rendering and argue for
the interpretation openly.

### Looking on the bodies is an event, not a permanent posture

The scene in Isaiah describes the righteous going out and looking. That the
looking is described at one point does not mean the moment is frozen for ever, so
that the onlookers are always looking or the bodies are always in that exact
state. Isaiah 66:23 has all flesh coming to worship before the Lord, and then
"they shall go out and look on" follows. Worshipping before the Lord and going
out to look are two different locations, so this is probably something done once
rather than for ever. That reading is offered as probable rather than certain.

### Even undying worms would not require an undying person

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the worms and the fire do last for ever.
The author thinks that unlikely on the wording and the context, but grants it as a
hypothetical. Even then, it would not follow that the person has to remain alive.

Worms that exist for ever would have to be sustained by God whether their food
source were dead or alive, because a worm that consumes for ever either consumes
nothing or has whatever it consumes continually replaced. Their continuance says
something about God sustaining worms; it says nothing about whether the body is
conscious.

The same holds for the fire. On the picture Revelation gives, the wicked are not
the fuel: the lake of fire is already burning before anything is thrown into it. A
fire that goes on burning does not require the wicked to persist consciously.

This is an argument in the alternative. It does not claim the worms and fire do
last for ever. It observes that even granting the objection in its strongest form,
the conclusion about a conscious person does not follow.

## Daniel 12:2

<Scripture reference="Daniel 12:2" />

Three observations from the source document bear on this verse.

The word rendered "shame" here is more commonly rendered "reproach." The
underlying Hebrew word is <Hebrew text="חֶרְפָּה" transliteration="cherpah" gloss="reproach, shame" />.
Reproach and contempt are both attitudes held toward something. They describe how
a person is regarded, not what a person feels. The comparison the source draws is
that a name can carry lasting contempt long after the person is dead.

The word rendered "contempt" in Daniel 12:2 is, on the source's reading, the same
word rendered "loathsome" or "abhorrence" of the dead bodies in Isaiah 66:24. If
that is right, the two verses are not merely similar in theme. They share
vocabulary, and in Isaiah that vocabulary is used of corpses. See also Jeremiah
23:40 for lasting reproach attached to those who are judged.

Joseph Dear argues that the Hebrew word for life used in Daniel 12:2 carries the
basic idea of being alive rather than the fuller idea of life in its richest
sense, which would mean that those raised to shame and everlasting contempt do
not go on being alive for ever <Cite id="dear-bible-teaches-annihilationism" locator="page 262" />.
That is a lexical argument reported here from its author rather than independently
verified.

<Callout tone="caution" title="How far the Daniel argument reaches" as="h3">
Daniel 12:2 sets everlasting life against shame and everlasting contempt. What
the verse plainly establishes is that both groups are raised and that the second
group's condition is described by how they are regarded. It does not by itself
settle how long the second group remains conscious. The observation that a person
need not be alive to be an object of contempt shows that the traditional reading
is not required by the verse, which is a weaker claim than showing it is excluded.
</Callout>

## What this argument establishes

- Mark 9:48 is a direct quotation of Isaiah 66:24, where the objects of the worm
  and the fire are dead bodies.
- Unquenchable fire in the Hebrew prophets describes fire that cannot be put out,
  and in every instance the thing burning is destroyed.
- Neither Isaiah nor Jesus describes the objects of the worm and the fire as
  conscious.
- Even if the worm and the fire were everlasting, that would not by itself
  require the person to remain alive.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That final punishment involves no conscious experience at all. Mark 9 is a
  warning of the gravest possible loss, and nothing here makes that loss mild.
- That every question about Gehenna in the Gospels is settled by this passage.
- That Daniel 12:2 positively teaches conditional immortality. It is compatible
  with it; that is a different claim.
