## In brief

The New Testament rarely describes the fate of the lost on its own. It usually names
it as the alternative to eternal life, in a sentence that offers two outcomes and
tells the reader which one is which. That habit gives us a useful list, because it
shows what the writers reached for when they needed a word to stand opposite life.

The author’s tally comes to ten expressions. Nine of them are the language of losing
life, being ended, or being sent away. The tenth is eternal punishment, and it is the
only one that the traditional reading takes to describe life continuing. The argument
of this page is that making the tenth mean unending conscious life leaves it as the
odd one out, and requires the other nine to be brought into line with it rather than
the other way round.

## Why traditional interpreters read the last one differently

Defenders of eternal conscious torment (ECT) do not deny that most of these words are
the language of loss. Their case rests on the observation that the last one is stated
in a strict parallel with eternal life, in a single sentence, with the same adjective
governing both halves. If the life is consciously enjoyed without end, they argue, the
punishment is most naturally consciously endured without end, or the parallel does no
work.

On that reading the other nine expressions are not being redefined so much as read in
the light of the clearest statement. Perishing, destruction and death are then names
for what it is to be finally and irreversibly lost, which is precisely what Matthew
25:46 says lasts for ever. Reading a whole vocabulary in the light of its most explicit
member is an ordinary interpretive move, not a special pleading, and this page should
not pretend otherwise.

## The conditionalist reading

Conditionalism accepts the parallel in Matthew 25:46 and affirms that the punishment is
eternal. What it disputes is the assumption that an eternal punishment must be an
eternal act of punishing rather than a punishment whose effect is eternal. That
argument is made in full in
[What Does “Eternal Punishment” Mean?](/case/key-texts/eternal-punishment/), including
the treatment of the adjective itself.

The point specific to this page is one of proportion. Nine expressions point one way.
If the tenth is read as unending conscious life, the other nine have to be adjusted to
match it: perish has to mean not to perish, destruction has to mean a destruction never
completed, death has to mean a kind of life, not having life has to mean having a life
of a certain kind. Each adjustment is defensible on its own. Taken together they amount
to a considerable amount of linguistic work, and all of it is needed to protect one
member of the list.

Read the other way, no adjustment is required anywhere. Eternal punishment takes its
sense from the company it keeps, and the whole set says one thing.

## The link between the first and the last

There is a connection between the first item on the list and the passage treated in
[Can God Destroy Both Body and Soul?](/case/biblical-language/body-and-soul/). The verb
rendered perish in John 3:16 is a form of the same verb rendered destroy in Matthew
10:28.

<Greek text="ἀπόλλυμι" transliteration="apollumi" gloss="destroy, kill, lose, ruin" />

That does not settle anything by itself, since the verb has a wide range and can mean
to lose or to ruin as well as to kill. What it does mean is that the best-known verse
in the Bible about the alternative to eternal life and Jesus’ statement about what God
can do to body and soul are using the same word. They are not two separate strands of
vocabulary that happen to point the same way.

<Callout tone="caution" title="Two limits on this list" as="h3">
The count of ten is the author’s own tally from his own reading. It is not drawn from a
published concordance study, and someone counting differently might reach a different
number or group the expressions differently.

The nine are also not all the same kind of expression. Perish, death, pass away, not
having life, not seeing life and destruction name an outcome. Wrath and fury,
condemnation, and depart from me name the judicial act, the verdict or the sentence of
exclusion rather than the state that follows. They belong on the list because they are
what the New Testament sets opposite eternal life, and because not one of them
describes life continuing. They should not be pressed as though all nine were synonyms
for ceasing to exist.
</Callout>

One further item deserves a note. 2 Corinthians 5:1 uses the destruction word of the
earthly tent, meaning the present body, rather than of the fate of the unrighteous. It
belongs in a list of places the vocabulary appears, but it is not itself a contrast
with eternal life, and no weight is placed on it here.

## What this argument establishes

- The stated alternative to eternal life in the New Testament is overwhelmingly the
  language of losing life.
- Not one of the ten expressions describes the life of the lost continuing.
- Reading eternal punishment as unending conscious life makes it the only member of the
  set that works that way, and requires the rest to be read in its light.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That any single one of these contrasts settles the question. None of them does.
- That the traditional reading of Matthew 25:46 is impossible. It is a reading with
  arguments behind it, answered in the page on that verse rather than here.
- That the tally of ten is exhaustive or authoritative.
