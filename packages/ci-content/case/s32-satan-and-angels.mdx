## In brief

Revelation 20:10 says the devil is thrown into the lake of fire and tormented
day and night forever and ever. Five verses later, human beings are thrown into
the same lake. If the same fire does two different things to two different
kinds of creature, that looks like special pleading, and the objection is that
conditionalists have simply carved out an exception to save the theory.

This is the point at which conditionalists genuinely disagree with one another,
and this page is written to show that rather than to hide it. The author of the
source document holds that the devil and his angels differ from human beings in
two ways that bear on judgment. Other conditionalists, including Joseph Dear,
hold that the devil is destroyed as well. The human case does not depend on
which of them is right.

## The objection, stated properly

<Scripture reference="Revelation 20:10" />

<Scripture reference="Matthew 25:41" />

The objection has two parts and both are strong.

First, defenders of the traditional view regard Revelation 20:10 as the
plainest statement in Scripture of unending conscious torment, and it is stated
of a person. Conditionalists spend a good
deal of effort arguing that fire in Scripture consumes, that destruction means
destruction, and that the second death is death. Revelation 20:10 is the one
verse that appears to say the opposite in plain words, and it is met by saying
that the creature in view is a different kind of creature. That is exactly the
move an objector would expect from a theory under pressure.

Second, Matthew 25:41 says the eternal fire was prepared for the devil and his
angels, and it is into that same prepared fire that human beings are sent. The
text does not present two fires with two functions. It presents one fire, and
it sends both parties into it. Defenders of eternal conscious torment (ECT)
therefore read Revelation 20:10 at face value for everyone who enters, and see
no warrant in the text for treating human beings differently.

## The author's two reasons for a distinction

### Angels are described as a different kind of being

Human beings are portrayed throughout Scripture as dependent creatures whose
continuing life is granted rather than owned. The tree of life stands at the
centre of that picture, in Genesis and again in Revelation, and access to it is
what sustains human life. Angels are never described this way. They are not
portrayed as needing the tree, and they are not described as being sustained by
it.

<Callout tone="caution" title="This is an inference, not a statement of Scripture" as="h3">
Scripture nowhere says directly that angels are inherently immortal. The
argument here is from silence and from difference of description: angels are
presented as a different order of creature, and nothing presents them as
dependent on the tree of life in the way humans are. That is a reason to be
cautious about assuming the two cases must run in parallel. It is not a proof
that they do not.
</Callout>

### They rebelled with unclouded knowledge

The second reason concerns culpability. Human rebellion happens under
conditions of limited sight.

<Scripture reference="1 Corinthians 13:12" />

Whatever we know of God, we do not see him directly, and every human being who
turns away does so at a distance and in partial light. Scripture does not
describe the fallen angels that way. They are presented as having had a clear
view of God and having rebelled anyway. Passages such as 2 Peter 2:4 and Jude 6
describe their fall as a decisive abandonment of a position they already held.
A different degree of knowledge at the point of rebellion is at least a
plausible reason for a different final outcome.

## The alternative conditionalist position

Some conditionalists do not accept this distinction, and their case deserves a
fair hearing rather than a footnote.

Their argument is that Revelation 20:10 belongs to a symbolic vision. Revelation
is saturated with symbolism throughout, and interpreters of every persuasion
read large parts of it figuratively. On this reading, the imagery of unending
torment for the devil, the beast and the false prophet functions within the
vision as a declaration of total and final defeat, and what it depicts in the
end is destruction. The devil is not an exception to the pattern; he is the
clearest case of it.

They also point to Paul.

<Scripture reference="1 Corinthians 15:24-26" />

If the end comes when every rival dominion, authority and power has been
destroyed, and the last enemy to be destroyed is death itself, then a devil
kept alive in conscious existence for ever sits awkwardly in that picture.
Joseph Dear develops this reading at length.

## The author's reply, and how firmly he holds it

The author does not think this reading is required, though he accepts that it
would fit 1 Corinthians 15:24-26 comfortably enough. His counter is that the
phrase in that passage is about dominion, authority and power, and those can be
destroyed without the devil personally being destroyed. His power, authority and
dominion can be stripped away completely while he himself remains. A nation can
lose all of its dominion, authority and power without every one of its citizens
being killed.

<Callout tone="author" title="From the author" as="h3">
The author records in the source document that this is the one area where he
holds his view loosely. He considers the question to have fewer immediate and
practical implications for how we live now than the main subject of the site,
which is why it receives less space, and he states that he is open to
correction on it. The alternative reading is presented above as a live option,
not as a view to be dismissed.
</Callout>

## Why the human case does not depend on this

It is worth being explicit about the scope of the disagreement, because it is
narrower than it looks.

Both conditionalist positions agree on what happens to human beings. Both hold
that the lost are raised, judged, and finally destroyed rather than kept alive
in torment. They differ only over whether one particular creature, of a
different order and a different history, shares that outcome or a different
one. Nothing in the biblical case for human conditional immortality, which
rests on the language of death, destruction and perishing across the whole
canon, turns on the answer.

Readers who find the author's distinction unpersuasive can reject it and hold
the rest of the case intact. That is not a concession made under pressure; it
is simply how the argument is built.

## What this argument establishes

- Conditionalists genuinely differ on the fate of Satan and the fallen angels,
  and this site does not present a united front where there is not one.
- There are reasons, of differing strength, for thinking the angelic case may
  not run parallel to the human case.
- The case for human conditional immortality does not depend on resolving the
  question either way.

### What it does not establish by itself

It does not establish that angels are inherently immortal, since Scripture does
not say so. It does not establish which of the two conditionalist readings of
Revelation 20:10 is correct. And it does not establish that the traditional
reading of that verse is mistaken; it argues only that the verse does not
settle the human question by itself.
