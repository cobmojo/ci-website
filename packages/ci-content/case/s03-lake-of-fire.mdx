## In brief

Revelation 20 contains the one sentence in Scripture that says torment continues
day and night for ever and ever. Everything turns on where that sentence sits.

It is in verse 10, at a point in the narrative where only three figures are in
the lake of fire: the devil, the beast and the false prophet. Five verses later
the unrighteous are thrown in, and the text does not repeat the clause. It names
the outcome differently. It calls the lake of fire the second death.

Eternal conscious torment (ECT), the view that the lost suffer consciously
without end, holds that those thrown into the same lake share the fate the
passage has just described. Conditional immortality (CI), the view that unending
life is given rather than innate, holds that the placement of the clause is
deliberate. This page sets out that argument and is candid about how much weight
it can carry, which is less than the argument's popularity sometimes suggests.

## Why ECT interpreters cite this passage

This is the passage where the traditional view is on its strongest ground, and
the case for it is straightforward.

The lake of fire is one place. Verse 10 says what happens in it: torment day and
night for ever and ever. Verse 15 then throws everyone whose name is not in the
book of life into that same lake. The text does not describe a second lake, or a
lake that behaves differently for different occupants. On the ordinary way of
reading a narrative, when the same destination is named twice the reader carries
forward what has been said about it.

The argument from placement is an argument from silence, and silence is weak
evidence. Verse 15 does not say the unrighteous are not tormented for ever; it says
nothing on the point. Reading a deliberate exclusion into that silence assumes John
was writing with the precision of a legal drafter.

The phrase "second death" does not settle the matter either. Whatever the second
death is, the beast and the false prophet are in it, and the passage says they are
tormented for ever. If being in the lake is compatible with continuing torment for
them, "second death" cannot by itself mean cessation of existence. Revelation 21:8
uses the same language of categories of people without suggesting a different
effect.

There is also a reading of Matthew 25:41 available to the traditional view that
does not make human beings an afterthought in a fire built for someone else. By
naming the devil, the worst evildoer there is, Jesus may be underlining that those
on his left will share the devil's fate. The author of the source document notes
this reading and says he does not hold it, but it is a natural sense of the verse
and many defenders of the traditional view take it.

Finally, Revelation 14:11 applies comparable imagery to human beings who worship
the beast, so a reader taking the two passages together does not need verse 15 to
repeat the clause.

## The conditionalist reading

### The lake was prepared for the devil and his angels

Matthew 25:41 says the eternal fire was prepared for the devil and his angels. It
does not say it was prepared for human beings. On the source's reading, this is
part of a wider pattern in which the righteous have a place prepared for them and
the unrighteous have none, so that being thrown into the fire is a way of being
thrown away rather than of being housed.

If the fire was prepared with the devil and his angels in view, it is not
unreasonable to expect that it does something to them that it does not do to
everything else put into it. The devil and his angels are spiritual beings of a
different order from human beings, who are not inherently immortal. A fire may
affect two different kinds of creature differently without any strain.

That reading is an inference, and the reasons the source gives for treating the
devil and his angels as deserving a more severe punishment are set out separately
in S32.

### Eternal fire in Jude describes a permanent effect

Jude says Sodom and Gomorrah serve as an example by undergoing a punishment of
eternal fire. The fire that fell on Sodom is not still burning. By the next
morning there was smoke over the plain and the cities were gone. What is eternal
about that fire is what it did, not how long it burned.

Cities destroyed by ordinary fire get rebuilt. Sodom and Gomorrah were destroyed
by what Jude calls eternal fire and were never rebuilt. The parallel passage in
2 Peter names what the example consists of:

<Scripture reference="2 Peter 2:6" />

And Genesis records what the site looked like the following morning:

<Scripture reference="Genesis 19:24-28" />

A further observation follows from ordinary experience. The only situation in which
we think of fire primarily as something that causes pain rather than as something
that destroys is a brief encounter from which we are then removed. Nothing is
removed from the lake of fire, so the consuming is inescapable and complete.

None of this requires the fire to stop burning. A perpetually burning fire makes
sense on the conditionalist view as well, since the devil and his angels are said
to be tormented there for ever and ever.

### The placement of the torment clause

Here is the central argument.

Consider what a reader would assume if verse 10 had not included the clause about
torment. Fire destroys. A reader coming to this passage without it would naturally
conclude that the devil, the beast, the false prophet, Death, Hades and those not
found in the book of life were all destroyed by the lake of fire. The clause is
inserted precisely because something atypical is happening: these three are not
destroyed.

The question is then whether the atypical effect covers everything thrown in
afterwards, or only the three named where the clause appears.

If the answer were everything, the clause could have been placed at the end of
verse 15, after the full list, and the point would have been unmistakable. It is
placed instead in verse 10, at the moment when only three figures are in the lake.

<Callout tone="note" title="An illustration, not an argument" as="h3">
The source document offers a small illustration to show the shape of the
inference. Imagine a host writing: I planned a party for two friends. They came.
They danced for a week straight. Two other guests also came to the party. Nothing
says the other two guests did not dance for a week, but the reader assumes they
did not, because the remarkable detail was attached to the first pair and
withheld from the second. Dancing for a week is not what happens at parties,
which is why it is worth mentioning at all. Being tormented for ever is not what
happens in a fire, which is why the clause is there. This illustrates the
reasoning; it does not establish it.
</Callout>

### What the text says instead when the others are thrown in

When Death and Hades are thrown into the lake, the passage does not repeat the
clause about torment. It gives a different description: this is the second death,
the lake of fire. On the source's reading, the narrative has moved on from the
atypical effect and is now describing what the lake does in the ordinary case.

That reading gains support from the promise a chapter later:

<Scripture reference="Revelation 21:4" />

If death shall be no more, and if the lake of fire is where Death and Hades were
thrown, then the lake destroyed them. This is the observation that carries the
most weight on this page. The lake of fire is not a place that preserves whatever
is put into it. Something was destroyed there. A place that destroys Death itself
is not naturally described as a place that keeps everything in existence for ever.

### One further observation

The rich man in Luke 16 is in torment before the last judgment. On the
traditional view, the lake of fire appears to reapply an experience he was
already having. The author's question is why the wicked would be raised and
judged only to be returned to the condition they were in before the resurrection.
That is a question rather than a demonstration, and the passage it depends on is
treated in S05.

## How much this argument can carry

An argument from placement is an argument from silence. It observes what the text
does not say at a point where it could have said it. That kind of argument is
suggestive and it is not decisive, and this page presents it as suggestive. It
shows that the traditional reading is not required by Revelation 20. It does not
show that the traditional reading is excluded by Revelation 20.

There is a further reason for caution. Revelation is full of symbolic images, and
the lake of fire may well be one of them. Death is thrown into it, and Death is
not a physical object that can be thrown anywhere. If the scene is symbolic, then
arguments that depend on the precise arrangement of who is in the lake at which
verse are working with material that may not be built to bear that kind of
weight. The author accepts this and notes that if the lake is symbolic, it is
also less likely that there is a physical or spiritual location where people
continue to exist.

The author's reading of the beast and the false prophet as spiritual entities
rather than human figures is his own present interpretation. He says openly that
he finds Revelation difficult at this point and does not present the reading as
settled. Other conditionalists disagree with him. Joseph Dear, whose book is the
most heavily used source behind this material, treats Revelation 20:10 as part of
a symbolic vision and holds that the devil is destroyed there as well

</Callout>

## What this argument establishes

- The clause about torment day and night for ever and ever is stated in verse 10,
  of three figures, before the unrighteous are thrown in.
- The lake of fire is described in Matthew 25:41 as prepared for the devil and
  his angels.
- Eternal fire in Jude 7 describes a fire whose effect is permanent, not one that
  is still burning.
- The lake of fire is named as the second death, and Death itself is destroyed
  there, so the lake is not a place that preserves what is put into it.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That the beast and the false prophet are certainly non-human figures. That is
  the author's own reading and other conditionalists reject it.
- That the lake of fire is a literal place rather than a symbolic image.
- The duration or intensity of what the unrighteous consciously experience there.
- That the traditional reading of verse 15 is impossible. An argument from
  placement cannot reach that conclusion.
