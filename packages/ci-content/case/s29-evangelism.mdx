## In brief

The objection is that conditional immortality (CI) lowers the stakes. If the
lost are finally destroyed rather than kept alive in torment forever, then the
worst thing that can happen to a person has a limit, and preachers who believe
that will feel less urgency, warn less plainly, and evangelise less. Some who
press this objection add that eternal conscious torment (ECT) has in fact
driven a great deal of missionary sacrifice, and that a doctrine which takes
the edge off that is dangerous whatever its exegetical merits.

That is a serious pastoral concern, held by serious people. This page gives two
replies: the first about method, the second about what actually motivates the
call to repentance in the New Testament.

## The methodological point comes first

Whether a doctrine helps or hinders evangelism has no bearing on whether it is
true.

This has to be said before anything else, because it decides the shape of the
whole discussion. Suppose someone could demonstrate beyond argument that
churches holding ECT evangelise more than churches holding CI. That would not
make ECT true. It would establish something about people, not something about
what Scripture teaches. The same cuts the other way: nothing on this site
treats a supposed pastoral advantage of conditionalism as evidence for it.

This site therefore makes no claim about what either doctrine does to
evangelistic activity in practice. No study measuring any such effect is cited
in the source document or on this page, and the question is left where it
belongs, outside the argument.

## The stakes are not small on this view

The objection assumes that a finite punishment is a mild one. It is not. On the
conditionalist reading the day of judgment is consciously experienced and
severe, and the outcome is the permanent loss of everything a person could have
had. A believer who takes that seriously has more than enough reason never to
grow cold or careless about the people around them. The impending judgment of
the lost is by itself sufficient to drive anyone to share the gospel.

## But judgment was never meant to be the main motive

Hell should not be the primary driver of evangelism. Eternal life with Jesus
should be.

Consider how the New Testament introduces the call to repentance.

<Scripture reference="Matthew 4:17" />

The stated ground of the call is that the kingdom is near, that it is now
accessible. The reason given is an opportunity, not a threat.

It is worth being precise here rather than overstating the point. Warnings of
coming judgment are present in the New Testament, including in the surrounding
chapters of Matthew, and this page does not claim otherwise. The narrower and
sounder claim is this: in the summary Matthew gives of Jesus' own preaching,
the reason attached to the call is the nearness of the kingdom. That is what
gets named.

### The dinghy and the cruise ship

Picture people adrift in the middle of the ocean in a sinking dinghy. One way
to call them across is to tell them that a magnificent cruise ship is alongside
and its captain is welcoming them aboard. Another way is to tell them to get
out of the water because of the sharks.

The second appeal is not false. The sharks are real. But it makes fear the
whole content of the invitation, and it leaves the hearer with an oddly
confused picture of the one doing the inviting, since on that framing the same
captain made the water and its dangers and permitted the hole in the boat. An
invitation built almost entirely on the terror of the alternative teaches
something about God whether or not the preacher intends it.

Eternity with God is not good primarily because it is better than the
alternative. It is good primarily because of who he is. That would be true if
there were no alternative to compare it with at all.

## God's own stated desire

If we are asking what should drive our evangelism, it is worth asking what
drives God's.

<Scripture reference="Matthew 23:37" />

Not one of these grounds the appeal in the horror of the alternative. Each
grounds it in God wanting people to live. If that is his heart, it should be
ours, and an evangelism shaped by it will not be short of urgency.

## What this argument establishes

- The consequences of holding a doctrine, good or bad, are not evidence for or
  against its truth.
- The New Testament attaches the call to repentance to the nearness of the
  kingdom and to God's desire that people should live.
- Conditionalism leaves the stakes absolute, since what is lost is everything.

### What it does not establish by itself

It does not establish that conditionalism produces more evangelism, or less.
No measurement of that is offered here, and no measurement of it would settle
which doctrine Scripture teaches. This page is a pastoral reflection rather
than a piece of the biblical case, and it is filed on this site as a further
consideration for that reason.
