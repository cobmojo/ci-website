## In brief

Conditional immortality (CI), the view that unending life is a gift given to the
redeemed rather than a property everyone already possesses, does not deny that the
punishment of the lost is eternal. It affirms it. The disagreement with eternal
conscious torment (ECT), the view that the lost suffer consciously without end, is
not about the word "eternal" but about what the noun attached to it names. Matthew
25:46 says eternal punishment, not eternal punishing. 2 Thessalonians says the
punishment is destruction. And every other thing the New Testament calls eternal is
accomplished once, with the result standing for ever.

## The primary passages

<Scripture reference="Matthew 25:46" />

<Scripture reference="2 Thessalonians 1:5-10" />

Verse 9 does the decisive work. It tells us what the punishment of Matthew 25:46
consists of: destruction.

## Why ECT interpreters cite these passages

The traditional argument from Matthew 25:46 is one of the best in this debate and
should be stated properly.

The verse sets two destinies in one sentence, in deliberate parallel, using the same
adjective for both. Nothing signals that the adjective is doing different work in
each half. If "eternal" in the second half means a conscious existence enjoyed
without end, consistency requires that it means a conscious existence endured without
end in the first. Reading one word two ways in a single sentence is what an
interpreter would object to if the other side did it.

The argument is often put in a harder second form. If eternal punishment does not go
on for ever, then its parallel, eternal life, does not either. No Christian accepts
the second, so the first must be rejected.

The noun and verb distinction is not decisive on its own, and the traditional view
has a reasonable answer to it. "Punishment" can name a continuing condition, as
"imprisonment" does, so Jesus saying punishment rather than punishing does not by
itself establish a completed act. The context supports the same reading: the goats
are sent into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels, and the
devil's own fate in Revelation 20:10 is torment for ever and ever.

As for 2 Thessalonians 1:9, widely used modern renderings read "away from the
presence" (ESV) and "shut out from the presence" (NIV). On that wording Paul appears
to describe where the lost are during their everlasting punishment. If they are
somewhere for eternity, they exist, and it would be strange to specify that people
are away from God's presence if they were not there at all. "Destruction" must then
describe something that happens to a person who goes on existing.

## The conditionalist reading

### Both halves are eternal, and CI affirms it

CI agrees that eternal punishment lasts for ever and ever exactly as eternal life
does. It never changes and is never reversed. What CI rejects is a smuggled premise:
that if eternal life is experienced consciously for ever, eternal punishment must be
experienced consciously for ever. That follows not from the text but from an
assumption about what the punishment is. Set out plainly, Matthew 25:46 fits the
conditionalist reading without straining the word "eternal":

- Are the righteous in Christ given life? Yes.
- Does that life last for ever and ever? Yes. That is eternal life.
- Are the wicked punished with death? Yes.
- Are they dead for ever and ever? Yes. That is eternal punishment.

### The nouns name results, not activities

The words in both verses are nouns. Punishment, not punishing. Destruction, not
destroying. Verbs are what the traditional reading needs, and verbs are not what the
text supplies. "Eternal destroying" would be hard to state coherently: if something
keeps existing for eternity, perpetually in the process of being destroyed, it is
never actually destroyed, because once destroyed it is no longer the thing it was. On
that reading God never finally destroys the wicked at all, which sits badly with the
many passages saying he does.

The English word "everlasting", used by some translations here, has drifted toward
the sense of actively ongoing for ever; "permanent" would often convey the sense more
accurately today. The Greek adjective behind these renderings,
<Greek text="αἰώνιος" transliteration="aionios" gloss="eternal, everlasting" />,
frequently does carry unending duration, and this page does not dispute that. Some
argue it can also carry a qualitative sense of belonging to the age to come; that is
contested and no weight is placed on it here.

### Everything else the New Testament calls eternal

The strongest form of the argument is about usage rather than grammar in the
abstract. In every other case, "eternal ___" names something done once whose result
stands for ever.

<Scripture reference="Hebrews 5:9" />

The saving happened once. The salvation stands for ever.

<Scripture reference="Hebrews 9:12" />

The redeeming happened once, by his own blood. The redemption stands for ever.
Hebrews 9:15 speaks the same way of the promised eternal inheritance: the
inheriting happens once and the inheritance stands.

<Scripture reference="Hebrews 6:2" />

Eternal judgment is the clearest case. Nobody supposes God spends eternity
adjudicating cases. The judging happens and the judgment stands.

<Scripture reference="Mark 3:29" />

An eternal sin is not a sin perpetually being committed. It is a sin that is
permanent and irreversible.

The pattern is consistent, and eternal punishment falls into it. The punishing
happens once. The punishment stands for ever.

### The problem with defining eternal life as endless living

If eternal life means simply living that goes on for ever, the traditional view runs
into a difficulty of its own making. On that view the lost are conscious and alive
and persist in that state for ever, so they too have living that goes on for ever.
The traditional view must therefore say that eternal life means more than endless
existence, since on its own account the lost have endless existence as well.

That concession has a consequence. If eternal life does not mean simply living that
goes on for ever, then eternal punishment does not mean simply punishing that goes on
for ever. The parallel cannot be used in one direction only.

Both sides can agree on the positive point. Eternal life is about the quality and
permanence of the life the righteous enter, not merely its length:

<Scripture reference="John 17:3" />

Following the pattern of the other eternal things, it could be described as fullness
of life given at one time, with the result standing for ever.

### John 10:10: life and fullness of life are two gifts

The difficulty remains whichever definition is adopted, because of this verse:

<Scripture reference="John 10:10" />

Jesus names two things, not one. Being alive is one gift; the fullness of that life
is another. By stating both separately, the verse treats existence and quality of
existence as distinct. The lost receive neither from Christ, so they do not have
existence for ever, and a horrid eternal life in hell is not what awaits them.

That inference is the author's, and it depends on reading the two clauses as naming
two distinct gifts rather than one idea stated twice. The wider evidence points the
same way: where the New Testament names the alternative to eternal life, the great
majority of those alternatives use the language of losing life. That survey,
including the author's own tally, is S11.

## What 2 Thessalonians 1:9 says the punishment is

Whatever else is disputed, the verse identifies the punishment. It is destruction,
not torment.

### The translation question

Standard translations divide on the preposition:

- NKJV: "from the presence" of the Lord
- ESV: "away from the presence" of the Lord
- NIV: "shut out from the presence" of the Lord

The author states plainly that he is unsure which is correct, and observes that each
is compatible with the conditionalist reading.

### If "away from" is right

The conditionalist answer on that wording is that the phrase does not identify where
the wicked are located after their destruction. It names what causes the destruction,
since God is the source of life. There is a foreshadowing behind that reading: when
Adam and Eve were shut out of the garden, they were shut out of the place where God
walked with them and away from the tree of life, and they subsequently died. Being
cut off from the source of life is not a stable condition; it is a cause of death.

It also creates a tension for one common use of Revelation 14:11, often read as
unending torment in the presence of the Lamb. The same people cannot be for ever both
in his presence and away from it.

### The author's own suggested reading

The author's preference, offered as a suggestion rather than a finding, is that the
NKJV is closer and that the preposition marks the source of the destruction rather
than a location. The word is
<Greek text="ἀπό" transliteration="apo" gloss="from, away from, by reason of" />.
He notes that this preposition commonly denotes that what follows is the source,
origin or starting point, and that it is most often rendered simply "from" unless
another word modifies it, which he does not find here
<Cite id="biblehub-apo-575" />. He also observes that the NIV's "and" has no
counterpart in the Greek and has been supplied.

His proposed sense, in his own words, is that "the source of their eternal
destruction is the presence of the Lord and the glory of his might." He labels this
his suggested translation, writes that he is "no Greek scholar", and says it "with
utmost hesitancy and humility". This site presents it on the same terms: as the
author's suggestion, not as a translation.

<Callout tone="caution" title="Specialist review needed" as="h3">
Three claims here need review by someone with formal training in Greek before this
page states them more strongly. First, that the preposition marks source rather than
separation: a concordance entry shows a word's range of use, is not a grammar, and
cannot settle how a preposition functions in a particular clause. Second, that the
NIV has supplied a conjunction with no counterpart in the Greek. Third, the claim in
the next section that a word pair occurs together in only two places. That standard
translations divide here is itself evidence that the question is not simple. The
conditionalist reading does not depend on winning this point: the verse says the
punishment is destruction on all three renderings.
</Callout>

### The presence that consumes

If the presence of the Lord is what destroys, the Old Testament supplies the
background:

<Scripture reference="Nahum 1:6" />

The same note runs through the Old Testament encounters with God, where the recurring
reaction is fear of not surviving. Israel at Sinai asks why they should die, saying
the great fire will consume them if they hear the voice of the living God any longer
(Deuteronomy 5:25-26).

There is also a difficulty with reading the verse as a permanent location. After the
judgment, God's presence is in the city, and the unrighteous are not admitted to it,
so they cannot go on existing for ever in his presence. On this reading they are
destroyed in a finite time in his presence and do not persist after that.

### The link with Isaiah 66

2 Thessalonians 1:7-8 describes the Lord Jesus revealed from heaven in flaming fire,
inflicting vengeance. Isaiah 66:15 pairs the same two ideas:

<Scripture reference="Isaiah 66:15" />

The source document makes the stronger claim that this combination occurs together
only in these two places. That is a statistical claim about the Greek text which this
page has not verified, and it is flagged in the callout above. What can be said
without overstating is that the two passages pair the same ideas closely, and that
Isaiah 66 goes on, nine verses later, to the corpses of Isaiah 66:24. If Paul is
drawing on Isaiah 66, the outcome he has in view looks like the one Isaiah describes.

## Is annihilation really an eternal punishment?

The objection deserves stating: if the lost cease to exist, they are no longer in
pain, so how is that punishment? The fuller answer is S30. The short answer is that
a punishment can be eternal by being final, definitive and unchanging rather than by
being continuously administered. For creatures made to live for ever in the presence
of the Lord, the permanent loss of that life is not a light thing.

## What this argument establishes

- The words in Matthew 25:46 and 2 Thessalonians 1:9 are nouns naming results, not
  verbs naming continuing activities.
- Every other "eternal ___" construction in the New Testament names something
  accomplished once whose effect stands permanently.
- 2 Thessalonians 1:9 identifies the punishment itself as destruction.
- If eternal life meant merely existing without end, the traditional view would have
  the lost enjoying that too, so it must read eternal life as more than that.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That the Greek adjective cannot mean everlasting duration. It often does.
- The correct rendering of the preposition in 2 Thessalonians 1:9. Standard
  translations divide and the author does not claim to have settled it.
- That the traditional reading of the parallel in Matthew 25:46 is incoherent. It is
  a serious argument requiring a serious answer.
