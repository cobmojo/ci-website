## In brief

Jesus died for us. That sentence is the centre of the New Testament's account of
salvation, and it is stated in those terms again and again. If his death stood in the
place of something, the thing it stood in the place of was death.

He also suffered, terribly, and nothing here treats that as incidental. The question is
narrower. If the penalty facing the unrighteous is unending conscious suffering, then
what paid for sin was the suffering of Christ, and his death becomes difficult to
account for. If what paid for sin was his death, then death is what the unrighteous were
facing.

The two positions in view are eternal conscious torment (ECT), that the unrighteous
suffer consciously and without end, and conditional immortality (CI), that immortality is
given in Christ and the unrighteous are finally destroyed. The strongest ECT reply to
this argument is stated below before it is answered.

Romans 3:25 is worth reading slowly. Paul says that God publicly set Christ forth as the
means of atonement, and he specifies how: by his blood. On the principle set out at
[What the Sacrificial System Says About Sin's Penalty](/case/biblical-patterns/sacrificial-system/),
blood makes atonement because it carries the life. The sins previously passed over were
not answered by a quantity of pain. They were answered by a life given up.

## The substitution argument

Set out plainly, the argument has three steps.

1. Scripture repeatedly says that Christ died for us, for our sins, in our place.
2. A substitute bears what those he stands for would otherwise have borne.
3. Therefore what those he stands for would otherwise have borne is death.

The argument does not require that the death was the only thing Christ endured, or that
his suffering was pointless. It requires only that the death was not incidental to the
payment, and the New Testament nowhere treats it as incidental.

## The reply from the infinite worth of Christ

The traditional answer is a serious one and deserves to be stated at full strength.

Christ is not merely a man. He is the eternal Son, of infinite dignity and worth, and
what he offers on the cross therefore has an infinite value that no created substitute
could supply. A penalty of infinite weight can be borne in a finite span by a person of
infinite worth, exactly as a debt of enormous size can be discharged in a moment by
someone of enormous means. So the fact that Jesus did not suffer for ever proves nothing
about the duration of the penalty he bore. Further, on this account his death is not an
awkward extra: death is the outward and public form his bearing of wrath took, the point
at which the endurance of the curse was completed. The traditional view can therefore
affirm both that he suffered our punishment and that he died our death, without treating
either as spare.

## Two problems with that reply

**If suffering satisfied the penalty, the death is hard to explain.** Suppose the
sufficiency lies in what Christ endured. Then once enough had been endured, the debt was
paid, and there was no remaining reason for him to die. Yet the death is precisely what
the apostolic summaries fix on. Paul's compressed statement of the gospel he received
begins with the death, not the scourging. Something the New Testament makes central has
become, on this reading, a demonstration attached to a payment already complete.

**If infinite worth compresses the penalty, it is not clear where the compression stops.**
The reply works by dividing an infinite penalty by an infinite dignity. But that
operation has no natural stopping point at the hours of the cross. If the worth of the
person is what makes a finite suffering sufficient, a far smaller suffering ought to have
been sufficient too. The source document puts the point sharply: on that logic, why not
a papercut? The question is rhetorical, but the difficulty behind it is real. An account
of the atonement that makes the quantity of suffering do the work has to explain why that
quantity, and the appeal to infinite worth removes the only obvious answer.

Neither point is a knock-down argument, and defenders of the traditional view have
answers to both. What the two together suggest is that the simplest reading of the
apostolic language, the one that needs no compression at all, is that Christ died the
death the unrighteous were facing.

## Genesis 22 and the shape of the substitution

<Scripture reference="Genesis 22:1-14" />

Abraham was told to offer Isaac. He was not told to make him suffer, and no part of the
narrative concerns pain. A ram is provided and dies in Isaac's place. Christians have long
read this as a foreshadowing of the Father and the Son, and what it foreshadows is a
substitutionary death. There is no torment in the picture at all.

## A suggestion from the author

<Callout tone="author" title="This is the author's own inference" as="h3">
What follows is offered by the author of the source document as a side idea, not as an
argument from a text. It is a theological inference drawn from a pattern he sees, and
it is presented here as such. Nothing in the case for conditional immortality depends on
it, and a reader who finds it unpersuasive can set it aside without loss.
</Callout>

The suggestion has two halves.

### The life from baptism to transfiguration

The author observes that the stretch of Jesus' life from his baptism to the
transfiguration traces, in physical events, the shape of a believer's spiritual life.

- He is baptised, and the Father speaks from heaven to affirm him as his Son
  (Matthew 3:17). The author notes that this is what God says of a person at the moment
  of new birth.
- The Holy Spirit comes upon him.
- He meets temptation and overcomes it by the word of God.
- He works in the power of the Spirit.
- He is transfigured to glory in an instant.

### The final day

If the first half of the pattern holds, the author suggests the last day of Jesus' life
traces the shape of the day of judgment for the unrighteous. He notes that Jesus speaks
of a cup he must drink (Matthew 26:39), and that the cup of God's wrath is the image used
of the unrighteous in Revelation 14:10, which is treated at
[Revelation 14: The Smoke of Their Torment](/case/key-texts/revelation-14/).

The sequence he draws out runs like this.

- A trial, at which a list of things done is set out. In Jesus' case, things he had not
  done.
- A conviction, carrying a sentence of death.
- Physical suffering, severe and brief.
- Spiritual suffering in separation from God, at the cry of Matthew 27:46, with darkness
  over the land. The author compares the outer darkness of the judgment parables, treated
  at [Weeping, Gnashing of Teeth, and Outer Darkness](/case/key-texts/weeping-and-gnashing/).
- A cry of thirst, which he compares with the rich man in Hades at Luke 16:24.
- Death, with breath failing.

<Scripture reference="Matthew 27:46" />

On the last item the author draws a further inference: that a creature separated from God
will not long have breath in it, since breath was his gift in the first place, as at
Genesis 2:7. One narrowing is needed. The Gospels do not state a physiological cause of
death. The point rests on the loss of breath, which they do describe, and not on a medical
account of how it was lost.

### What the picture does not claim

The author states this explicitly, and it must not be lost. **Jesus was not annihilated.**
His soul continued past his death. He did not need to be annihilated for the picture to
hold, because what is doing the work is his physical experience, and physical death is
what points to the ultimate loss of life the unrighteous face in body and soul. The
picture is an analogy running from the physical to the spiritual. It is not a claim that
the same thing happened to him.

## What this argument establishes

- Scripture consistently identifies the death of Christ, not merely his suffering, as
  what saves.
- Romans 3:25 locates the answer to sins previously passed over in the shedding of blood,
  which is the giving of a life.
- The binding of Isaac foreshadows a substitutionary death rather than a substitutionary
  torment.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That the suffering of Christ was incidental to the atonement. It was not, and no
  conditionalist argument requires that it was.
- That the reply from the infinite worth of Christ has no answer. It is a coherent
  position held by careful theologians, and the two problems above are difficulties
  rather than refutations.
- That Christ was annihilated. He was not, and the page says so directly.
- The reading of the final day as a picture of judgment establishes nothing on its own.
  It is labelled an inference by the author and treated as one here.
