## In brief

Genesis does something unusual: it tells us why God barred the way back into the garden.
The reason given is that otherwise the man would reach out, take from the tree of life,
eat, and live for ever. Access to the tree is connected in the text itself to continuing
to live.

The tree reappears at the end of Revelation. It stands inside the city, on either side of
the river. Those who wash their robes have the right to it and enter by the gates. Others
are outside, and the text names them.

Put together, the two scenes describe continued life as something granted and guarded
rather than something possessed. That is the heart of conditional immortality (CI), the
view that endless life is a gift given in Christ rather than a property every human being
holds by nature. Eternal conscious torment (ECT) is the view that the finally unrighteous
exist for ever in conscious suffering.

One step in the usual conditionalist presentation of this argument is a theological
inference rather than something Scripture states, and this page marks it clearly. The
main argument does not depend on it.

## The tree in Genesis

<Scripture reference="Genesis 2:9" />

<Scripture reference="Genesis 3:22-24" />

Two things stand in the garden: the tree, and the presence of the Lord. On the day the
man and the woman ate from the other tree, they hid themselves, and the relationship they
had enjoyed with God was broken. God then removed them from the garden and set a guard on
the way to the tree of life.

The source document reads these as two kinds of sustenance, physical and spiritual, and
reads the expulsion as a severing of both. That framing is a reading of the narrative
rather than something Genesis states in those terms, and it should be held as such. What
Genesis does state is the reason for the guard, and the reason is about living for ever.

## The reason God gives

This is the load-bearing point. Scripture rarely explains God's motives, and here it
does. The concern is not that the man would enjoy the tree's benefits or its beauty. The
concern named is that he would eat and live for ever.

That connects the tree to the continuation of life, not merely to the quality of life.
And what follows in the narrative matches: outside the garden the man works the ground
he was taken from, and in due course returns to it. Their bodies became mortal in the
plain sense that they eventually died.

## The tree in Revelation

<Scripture reference="Revelation 2:7" />

<Scripture reference="Revelation 22:1-5" />

<Scripture reference="Revelation 22:14-15" />

Three features of these texts matter for the argument. The tree is promised as a reward
to the one who conquers. The tree is located inside the city, in the same place as the
throne and the presence of God, exactly as it was located with God's presence in the
garden. And access to it is stated as a right belonging to a defined group, with others
described as outside.

Whether the people described as outside in Revelation 22:15 are actually present at that
moment, or whether the verse is a recapitulation addressed to present readers, is
disputed. That question is taken up in
[S28, on whether the wicked are still present after Revelation 20](/objections/revelation-after-20-15/).
The argument on this page works either way, because it turns on who has access to the
tree rather than on who is standing where.

## How ECT interpreters read the tree

The traditional reading is not that the tree is unimportant. It is that the tree is about
blessing rather than about bare existence.

On this reading, the tree of life signifies the fullness of life in God's presence: life
in the qualitative, covenantal sense that runs through Scripture, where to live is to be
in fellowship with God and to die is to be cut off from him. Exclusion from the tree is
therefore exclusion from blessedness, not from being. Defenders of the traditional view
can point out that Revelation 22:2 speaks of the leaves of the tree healing the nations,
which is language about flourishing, and that Revelation itself describes the second
death in terms of a state rather than a cessation. They can also point out that a symbol
in an apocalyptic vision is a poor basis for a claim about the mechanics of created
existence.

Many who hold ECT would add a further answer. God raises all the dead, righteous and
unrighteous alike, for judgment. If God's own act of resurrection gives the unrighteous a
body that endures, then their continued existence does not depend on the tree at all, and
their exclusion from it says nothing about whether they persist.

## The conditionalist response

The conditionalist reply to the first point is short, and it is the reason this argument
is made at all. Genesis supplies the reason for the exclusion, and the reason is stated in
terms of living for ever. Scripture is not silent here and left to be filled in by a
theory of what life language means elsewhere. The stated concern is continued life. That
does not exclude the qualitative sense of life; it does mean the qualitative sense cannot
be the whole of it.

The reply to the second point is that Scripture describes an imperishable resurrection
body for those raised in Christ, and never says the unrighteous receive one. That case is
made in
[S22, on conditional immortality](/case/final-destiny/conditional-immortality/), and it is
the argument this page leans on rather than one it establishes.

## The step that is inference, not exegesis

The source document says that in the new creation we will apparently continue to eat from
the tree in order to sustain our bodies. That word apparently is the right one, and it is
preserved here.

**This is a theological inference, not a statement Scripture makes.** Revelation says the
tree is there, that it bears fruit and that its leaves heal the nations. It does not say
that resurrected bodies would fail without it, and it is entirely possible that the tree
is an image of the life God gives rather than a mechanism by which he gives it.

**The exclusion argument does not depend on that inference being correct.** The argument
is that God guarded the tree in Genesis for a stated reason connected with living for
ever, and that in Revelation the tree stands inside a city from which some are excluded.
Both of those are things the text says. Whether or not the redeemed are sustained by
eating from it, the pattern of guarded access to unending life remains, and the
unrighteous are on the wrong side of the guard.

## The question the source presses

The source document ends this section with a question. If God would not permit Adam and
Eve to live for ever in their fallen condition, why would he grant the wicked unending
existence in conscious torment?

It is a question rather than a demonstration, and the traditional view has answers to it,
including the one noted above about resurrection. But it does put the matter in a useful
light. On the conditionalist reading, God's action in Genesis and God's action at the end
are the same action: unending life is his to give, it is bound up with his presence, and
it is given in Christ.

## What this argument establishes

- Genesis links access to the tree with living for ever, and gives that as the reason for
  the exclusion.
- In Revelation the tree stands inside the city, access to it is a right belonging to a
  defined group, and others are described as outside it.
- Continued life is presented across both scenes as something granted and guarded rather
  than something inherent.

### What it does not establish by itself

- The mechanics of how resurrected bodies are sustained. That is inference and is labelled
  as such above.
- That the tree is a literal tree rather than an image. The argument does not require an
  answer either way.
- That the qualitative sense of life is absent from these texts. It is present; the claim
  is that it is not the whole of what is meant.
