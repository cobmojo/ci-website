## In brief

When a person dies physically, they no longer consciously exist in the physical world.
If the physical is designed to picture the spiritual, the second death names the
corresponding loss in the spiritual realm.

The second move on this page matters more. Defenders of eternal conscious torment (ECT)
answer that death in Scripture means separation rather than cessation. Grant that
definition entirely, and the conclusion of conditional immortality (CI) still follows,
because of who the separation is from.

<Callout tone="note" title="What kind of argument this is" as="h3">
This page argues by inference from a pattern. Scripture does not say in so many words
that the second death is the spiritual counterpart of the first. It is offered as a
reading that fits, not as a proof text, and it is weaker than the direct arguments from
the language of destruction and from eternal punishment.
</Callout>

## The parallel

Ask what a dead body is like. There is no person in it. Conscious existence in this world
has ended for the one who has died. That is the one thing every human being already knows
about death.

Scripture then names a second death.

<Scripture reference="Revelation 20:14" />

If the first death pictures the second, the picture is not obscure. What physical death
is to this realm, the second death is to the realm beyond it.

## The ECT reading: death as separation

The traditional reply is that this trades on a mistaken definition. In Scripture, death is
not annihilation but separation. Physical death is the separation of the spirit from the
body, which James states directly.

<Scripture reference="James 2:26" />

Spiritual death is then the separation of a person from God, and Paul can describe living
people as dead in trespasses, at Ephesians 2:1, while they are plainly conscious and
active. The second death is the final and irreversible form of that separation: the person
continues,
consciously, cut off from God for ever. On this account the word death is used
consistently throughout, and it is the conditionalist who has read a modern sense into it.

## Why the separation definition leads to the same place

Take that definition and follow it. Separation from whom?

<Scripture reference="Colossians 1:17" />

<Scripture reference="Hebrews 1:3" />

Paul says the same thing at 1 Corinthians 8:6: all things are from the Father and through
the Son, and we exist through him. The New Testament does not present Christ as a source
of life in some restricted sense while creation carries on under its own power, but as the
one by whom everything continues to exist at all.

If that is so, a creature genuinely and finally separated from him is not a creature still
sustained. Being upheld and being cut off from the one who upholds are not two conditions
that can be held at once. The ECT reading needs the second death to be a separation that
leaves its subject intact, and the passages describing what Christ does for creation make
that combination hard to hold.

## What this establishes

- The separation definition of death does not by itself secure the continued existence of
  the one separated.
- Physical death is a natural picture of the loss that the phrase second death names.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That the parallel is exact in every respect. It is a pattern, not an equation.
- That God could not sustain a creature apart from fellowship with himself. The argument
  concerns what the language of separation implies, not divine ability.
- That the wicked simply cease with nothing experienced. Nothing on this site claims that
  judgment is painless or instant.
