## In brief

Romans 5 contains an explicit denial of symmetry. Paul says the gift is not like the
trespass, and then says something close to it twice more in the same few sentences.
Whatever else that means, it means the two sides of his comparison are not being offered
as matching opposites of equal weight in opposite directions.

That matters for how final punishment gets imagined. The intuition that hell must be the
exact counterpart of heaven, equally endless and equally extreme in the other direction,
is a theological assumption rather than a statement of Scripture. In the one place where
Paul deliberately sets sin and grace side by side, he refuses the straight comparison.

The claim here is narrow. Symmetry between the two destinies has to be argued for rather
than assumed and used as a premise. Nothing here claims that Romans 5 settles the nature
of final punishment, or that final punishment is light.

Throughout this site, conditional immortality (CI) is the view that endless life is a
gift given in Christ, and eternal conscious torment (ECT) is the view that the finally
unrighteous exist for ever in conscious suffering.

## The intuition this answers

Stated at its strongest, the case for symmetry runs like this.

Scripture puts two destinies before us and repeatedly uses parallel language for both.
In Matthew 25:46 the same word qualifies both the punishment and the life, inside a
single sentence. If the life is unending, the natural reading is that the punishment is
unending too. Add the moral intuition that a heaven beyond imagining implies an opposite
loss of comparable magnitude, and the result is a picture of two everlasting states, each
as far from ordinary experience as the other.

The source document labels this picture dualism, meaning the idea of two matched and
opposed eternities. It is held by careful and serious readers, and the parallel in
Matthew 25:46 is genuinely there. That verse is treated on its own terms in
[S04](/case/key-texts/eternal-punishment/), and nothing here disposes of it in passing.

## What Paul actually says

Paul is not answering a question about the duration of hell in Romans 5. He is arguing
that what came through Christ is greater than what came through Adam. But the way he
argues is the point. He could have drawn a tidy balance: one man's sin brought this
much ruin, one man's obedience brought exactly that much restoration. He explicitly
declines. He says outright that the gift and the trespass are not alike, and that the
gift of God cannot be set alongside the result of one man's sin as though the two were
comparable quantities. Judgment followed one offence; the gift followed many, and it
still overflows.

So when Scripture does put condemnation and life next to each other in an extended
comparison, the writer's own comment is that the two do not match. That is a poor
foundation for a doctrine built on the premise that they must.

## Where this leaves the two destinies

The conclusion available here is a modest one. The symmetry intuition is an inference
about how the two destinies ought to relate. It is not a proposition Scripture asserts,
and Romans 5 pushes in the other direction. Anyone who wants to reason from symmetry
should first establish it from the text rather than treat it as the obvious default.

### A side effect worth noticing

The source document adds an observation about what the symmetrical picture does to
Christian imagination. If hell is understood as the mirror image of heaven, then the more
a believer dwells on the greatness of heaven, the more that believer is obliged to
enlarge the horror of hell to match it. Since almost nobody wants to dwell on that, the
practical result can be that people avoid thinking carefully about eternity at all. This
is an observation about how the doctrine functions in practice, not an argument that it
is false. A doctrine can be uncomfortable and still be true, and
[the preface](/case/why-this-matters/) says plainly that the usefulness of a belief is
never evidence for it.

### The net-outcome argument

At this point the source document sets out a philosophical argument about the summed
outcome of humanity under each view, using Matthew 7:13-14 on the many and the few. The
source states in capitals that this is a purely philosophical argument which therefore
really holds no weight, because Scripture is what matters. That warning is the author's
own and it is preserved here. The full treatment, including the assumptions it depends on
and the problems with arithmetic of this kind, is set out in
[Appendix 2](/appendix/net-outcome-of-humanity/) and is not developed on this page.

## What this argument establishes

- Paul explicitly denies that the gift and the trespass are comparable in kind.
- Treating the two destinies as equal and opposite is a theological assumption rather
  than a biblical statement, and it needs its own support.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That final punishment is light. Asymmetry is not triviality, and CI holds that final
  judgment is consciously experienced, severe, and to be avoided at all costs.
- That Romans 5 tells us how long final punishment lasts. Paul is contrasting the reach
  of grace with the reach of sin, not measuring durations. Anyone who wants the duration
  question answered has to go to the passages that address it.
- That defenders of ECT rely on symmetry. Many argue from particular texts and would not
  put weight on the mirror-image picture at all.
