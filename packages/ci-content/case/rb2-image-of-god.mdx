## In brief

Bearing the image of God means that human beings reflect God in creaturely measure. It does not mean they share the attributes that belong to God alone. Scripture describes human life as dependent and fragile, and it names immortality as something God possesses and gives rather than something creatures own.

That is the biblical claim on this page, and it stands on its own. A second and quite different claim, about how the idea of an inherently immortal soul entered Christian thinking, is treated separately below, because it is a historical question and the evidence for it works differently.

## The traditional argument, stated fairly

Many defenders of eternal conscious torment (ECT) hold that human beings, uniquely among earthly creatures, were made for endless existence, and that this follows directly from being made in God's image. God does not create an image-bearer and then unmake it. On that view the question at the judgment is never whether a person continues, only where they continue, and annihilation would be a defeat for the Creator rather than a sentence from him.

That is a serious position and it is not an unreasonable inference. The question is whether Scripture actually draws it.

<Scripture reference="Genesis 1:26-27" />

The text says image and likeness. It does not say immortality, and no later passage supplies that definition.

## What the image is, and what it is not

Nobody holds that image-bearing transfers God's attributes wholesale. We are not omniscient, not omnipresent, not omnipotent. Human beings do reflect God in justice, compassion and love, but these are dull reflections of perfect attributes rather than shares in them. Scripture nowhere declares that every human being has an eternal spirit.

Joseph Dear presses the parallel that follows from this. Being made in God's image gives us a sense of justice and morality, yet without Christ we remain lost in sin and inclined toward evil. The image gives us the idea of righteousness because God is perfectly righteous, and yet we are not actually righteous as he is.

> Why could it not be that being made in God's image allows us to have an idea of immortality, since God is immortal, but because of sin, outside of His mercy and grace in Jesus Christ we are not actually immortal like the God whose image we bear?

<Cite id="dear-bible-teaches-annihilationism" />

The parallel does not prove the conclusion. It does show that the inference from image to immortality is not forced, because the same inference from image to righteousness is one nobody makes.

## How Scripture describes human life

The assumption running through Scripture is that human beings are not immortal. We are expected to die. The language is consistent and it is not flattering.

<Scripture reference="Psalms 144:4" />

<Scripture reference="James 4:14" />

<Scripture reference="Isaiah 40:6-8" />

Peter picks up that passage from Isaiah at a pointed moment, immediately after telling his readers what has changed for them.

<Scripture reference="1 Peter 1:23-24" />

The seed is imperishable and the word abides, and then the grass withers. The author reads the sequence as deliberate: you were the grass and the flower, temporary things, and in Christ you have been born of something that does not perish. He notes as an inference, and it is an inference rather than a statement of the text, that this cannot be describing their bodies, since the letter is written to people who have not yet received the resurrection body.

The body is on the same footing until then.

<Scripture reference="1 Corinthians 15:42-43" />

What is sown is perishable. What is raised is imperishable. That is a change, and a change implies a prior state that was otherwise.

## Where Scripture locates immortality

Having described human life as perishable, Scripture is direct about where immortality is actually found.

<Scripture reference="1 Timothy 6:15-16" />

Immortality is stated as belonging to God alone. It then appears as a gift.

<Scripture reference="2 Timothy 1:10" />

<Scripture reference="John 6:51" />

And it appears as something to be sought.

<Scripture reference="Romans 2:7" />

The author's question about that last verse is a fair one. Why would immortality be described as something sought after if human beings already possessed it by nature? In context it arrives bundled with glory and honour, and the unrighteous are not described as receiving either of those.

One passage is sometimes read the other way.

<Scripture reference="Ecclesiastes 3:11" />

Eternity set in the human heart is not a statement that human beings are eternal. It reads more naturally as a longing for, or an inclination toward, what is eternal. That is how the author takes it, and the same verse goes on to say that we cannot find out what God has done from beginning to end, which sits awkwardly with a claim about our own constitution.

## The historical claim, kept separate

The source document puts the historical point bluntly: the immortality of the soul is from Plato, not the Bible. Something real sits behind that. Greek philosophical accounts of the soul did shape later Christian anthropology, and traditional Christianity did come to hold a doctrine of the naturally immortal soul that the biblical writers do not state.

<Callout tone="caution" title="The history is more tangled than a single line of influence" as="h3">
Tracing the doctrine to Plato in one step is too simple to be accurate. Ideas about what survives death were already varied in Second Temple Judaism, the world in which the New Testament writers were formed, and those views were not uniform. Between Plato and the settled Christian doctrine lie several centuries of Christian reflection, in which theologians were arguing with Greek philosophy at least as often as they were borrowing from it, and in which the resurrection of the body was defended precisely against philosophical accounts of a soul escaping it.

The honest version of the claim is that Christian anthropology developed under substantial philosophical influence, over a long period, with the immortality of the soul becoming standard along the way. That is enough to say the doctrine needs defending from Scripture rather than assuming. It is not a demonstration that any particular writer took it from Plato, and this page does not assert one.
</Callout>

The biblical claim above does not depend on the historical one. If the history were fully reconstructed and turned out differently, 1 Timothy 6:15-16 would still say what it says.

## A denial from the beginning

The author ends where the question first arose. The first denial that human beings die was not made by God.

<Scripture reference="Genesis 3:4" />

The claim that human beings possess life independently of God, in his own words, is a lie from the beginning.

## What this argument establishes

- That Scripture nowhere states that the image of God confers inherent immortality.
- That immortality is attributed to God alone and described as a gift brought to light in Christ.
- That human existence is repeatedly described as dependent and perishable.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That human beings are merely physical, or that the intermediate state between death and judgment is unconscious. Nothing above settles that.
- How God sustains the unrighteous until the day of judgment. That he does is treated elsewhere; the mechanics are not described here.
- That every defender of ECT rests the case on the image of God. Many do not.
