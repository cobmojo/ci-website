## In brief

Revelation 21 promises that death, mourning, crying and pain will be no more, because the
former things have passed away. The question is what passing away means, and the passage
supplies its own control: three verses earlier the same verb is used of the first heaven
and the first earth.

Nobody reads that first use as relocation. The old order is not moved somewhere else and
kept running out of sight. If death and pain pass away in the same sense in which the
first heaven and the first earth pass away, then they do not continue elsewhere either.

Conditional immortality (CI) holds that unending life is a gift given in Christ; eternal
conscious torment (ECT) holds that the finally unrighteous exist for ever in conscious
suffering.

## The passage

<Scripture reference="Revelation 21:1-4" />

## How ECT interpreters read it

The traditional reading is that this is a promise, and promises have addressees.

John is describing the new creation and the people who dwell in it. The tears wiped away
are theirs. The pain that ends is theirs. On this reading the passage is not a survey of
every location in the created order; it is a description of what the city is like for
those inside it, given in the warmest terms Scripture has. Read that way, it says nothing
one way or the other about what is happening outside the city, and it would be an
overreach to make a promise of comfort do the work of a cosmological statement.

Defenders of the traditional view would add that Revelation trades in vision and image
throughout, and that pressing the logical extension of a comforting promise is not how
apocalyptic is meant to be read.

## The verb

The conditionalist response turns on a feature of the text rather than on the logic of
promises.

<Greek text="ἀπέρχομαι" transliteration="aperchomai" gloss="go away, depart, pass away" />

The same verb stands behind the passing away of the first heaven and the first earth in
verse 1 and the passing away of the former things in verse 4. The first use is not a
relocation. The first heaven and the first earth are not moved out of the way and
maintained elsewhere; they are gone, replaced by a new heaven and a new earth. Verse 4
gives the reason for the absence of death and mourning and crying and pain in exactly
those words: the former things have passed away.

So the question is not whether verse 4 is addressed to the redeemed. It plainly is. The
question is what grounds the promise, and the ground John gives is that the former things
have passed away in the same sense the old order has, which is not relocation.

Paul's language points the same direction. In 1 Corinthians 15:26 the last enemy to be
destroyed is death itself. An enemy that has been destroyed is not an enemy still
operating in another region.

Whether anyone is present outside the city in the first place is a separate question,
taken up in
[S28, on whether the wicked are still present after Revelation 20](/objections/revelation-after-20-15/).

## A pastoral observation, not an exegetical one

The source document raises a further question. On the traditional view, will the redeemed
remember the people they loved who are lost? It is hard to picture no more mourning or
crying if those people are consciously suffering without end.

**This is a pastoral observation rather than an argument from the text.** It reports how
the two pictures feel rather than what a passage says, and there are serious traditional
answers to it, including the answer that the redeemed will see God's justice as good and
will not grieve over what God has rightly done. Feelings about a doctrine are not evidence
either way, as [the preface](/case/why-this-matters/) says directly. The observation is
preserved here because it is in the source, and it is labelled for what it is.

## God takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked

<Scripture reference="Ezekiel 33:11" />

The source document draws an argument from lesser to greater: if God takes no pleasure in
the death of the wicked, how much less would he take pleasure in their unending conscious
torment.

Two things should be said about that. It is a genuine statement about God's disposition
toward judgment, and it belongs in any discussion of final punishment, because it rules
out any picture in which God relishes what he does to the wicked. But it is not a
discriminating argument between the two views, because defenders of eternal conscious
torment agree entirely that God takes no pleasure in it. The verse is an appeal to Israel
to repent and live, and both sides of this debate can and do make that appeal.

## What this argument establishes

- The same verb describes the passing of the first heaven and the first earth and the
  passing of death, mourning, crying and pain.
- Passing away in the sense the passage uses is not relocation.
- The reason John gives for the absence of these things is the passing of the former
  order, not the shielding of the redeemed from a state that continues elsewhere.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That the promise in Revelation 21:4 is addressed to anyone other than the redeemed. It
  is addressed to them.
- That the argument settles what happens outside the city, which depends on the separate
  question of whether anyone is there.
- Anything from Ezekiel 33:11 that the traditional view would dispute, since both views
  affirm it.
