## In brief

"There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth" is one of the most quoted phrases in
the whole debate, and it is worth asking what the two words mean. Gnashing of teeth
signals anger everywhere else it appears in Scripture, and weeping signals grief.

Eternal conscious torment (ECT), the view that the lost suffer consciously without
end, takes the phrase as a description of the settled condition of the lost.
Conditional immortality (CI), the view that unending life is given rather than
innate, agrees that the response is real and conscious, and observes that no text
says it continues without end. The phrase turns up in parables with a fiery furnace
and in parables with no fire at all, which points to the judgment scene rather than
to a place of ongoing burning.

## Why ECT interpreters cite this passage

The traditional reading has real force here and should be stated before it is
answered.

The phrase is Jesus' own, and he uses it repeatedly across different parables. A
formula repeated that often is not incidental colour; it is a settled way of
describing the condition of those shut out. Whatever the words mean, they describe
people who are conscious, aware of their situation and reacting to it.

In Matthew 13 the phrase follows the throwing into the furnace. The most natural
reading of a sentence in that order is that the weeping and gnashing happen after
the throwing, which is to say in the furnace. If the weeds were simply burned up and
that were the end of it, adding a description of conscious anguish afterwards would
be strange. Jesus adds it, and the traditional reading takes the addition seriously.

In Matthew 22 and Matthew 25 the phrase is attached to the outer darkness, and the
man is cast into it. "Cast into" describes a destination, and a destination is a
place where someone is, not a moment through which someone passes. Jude reinforces
the picture by saying the blackest darkness has been reserved for such people for
ever: a permanent condition and a location, named together.

## What the two words mean elsewhere

### Gnashing of teeth denotes anger

Every other occurrence of gnashing of teeth in Scripture is an expression of rage,
not of physical suffering.

<Scripture reference="Job 16:9" />

Job is describing what he takes to be God's anger and hostility toward him.

<Scripture reference="Psalms 35:16" />

David is describing his enemies.

<Scripture reference="Acts 7:54" />

The members of the council are furious with Stephen. They are not in pain; they
are enraged.

The most telling instance for this discussion pairs the gnashing with an outcome:

<Scripture reference="Psalms 112:10" />

The wicked see the righteous lifted up, gnash their teeth, and melt away. Whatever
"melt away" describes, it is not a condition that lasts for ever.

### Weeping denotes grief

The word rendered weeping,
<Greek text="κλαυθμός" transliteration="klauthmos" gloss="weeping, wailing" />,
carries sorrow rather than physical pain.

<Scripture reference="Matthew 2:18" />

Rachel weeps for her children and refuses to be comforted, because they are no more.

<Scripture reference="Acts 20:37" />

The Ephesian elders weep as Paul leaves them. Grief, not agony. Taken together, the
phrase reads most naturally as sorrow and anger rather than as pain or the reaction
to pain.

<Callout tone="note" title="If it does mean pain" as="h3">
The source document concedes this point rather than fighting it. If someone insists
that weeping and gnashing of teeth must denote pain rather than sorrow and anger,
that is entirely compatible with the conditionalist position. The wrath of God at the
judgment could induce severe pain of several kinds at once: physical, mental,
emotional and spiritual. Nothing in conditional immortality requires the judgment to
be gentle. The argument on this page is about duration, not severity.
</Callout>

## The phrase appears with fire and without it

This observation does the most work. The phrase occurs in a parable with a fiery
furnace (Matthew 13) and in parables with no fire mentioned at all (Luke 13,
Matthew 22, Matthew 25). If the weeping and gnashing were the reaction to being
burned, the occurrences without any fire would be out of place. The better account is
that the response is sorrow and anger at being cast away from the Lord's presence,
which is common to all these parables.

Two things follow. The phrase is not confined to the furnace and is not the primary
outcome of the furnace. And the traditional argument that the furnace does not
destroy the wicked, made on the ground that they go on weeping and gnashing
afterwards, is therefore not established by the text.

## Luke 13 and the question of "that place"

<Scripture reference="Luke 13:22-30" />

The scene describes the last judgment. The householder gets up, closes the door and
refuses to recognise those knocking. Just as the exchange in verses 25 to 27 happens
once rather than repeating for ever, the weeping and gnashing in verse 28 belongs to
that occasion, describing what happens as they are thrown out.

Then there is the phrase "in that place." Which place? The only place described in
the whole story is the house, and the house is where they are not admitted. There is
no second location in the parable, no place they are subsequently sent to, so "in
that place" is most naturally read as the judgment scene itself.

## Matthew 13 and the furnace

<Scripture reference="Matthew 13:24-43" />

The same phrase, "in that place," appears here, and the same reading applies: it most
likely refers to the whole scene of the last judgment rather than to the interior of
the furnace. The wording is identical to Luke 13:28, where there is no furnace at
all, and the parable of the net later in the same chapter (Matthew 13:47-50) closes
with the same formula. On either view of final punishment, weeping and gnashing would
naturally surround all the events of the last judgment rather than being confined to
what happens in the fire.

If a reader holds instead that the response must take place inside the furnace, the
conditionalist reading still stands. It is perfectly possible to be conscious and to
react while being burned up; nothing requires the fire to extinguish a person
instantly.

The author's own suggestion is that Jesus adds the clause in verse 42 to mark the
limits of the parable. Weeds burn without feeling anything; the unrighteous will have
a conscious response in the process. The clause guards against reading the parable as
though the end were a painless switching off. It does not suggest the unrighteous
fail to burn up, which would undo the point of choosing weeds at all.

## Matthew 22: the wedding feast

<Scripture reference="Matthew 22:1-14" />

This parable is the most instructive of the set, because it contains two different
punishments. Those who refused the invitation and mistreated the servants are dealt
with in verse 7: the king sends his troops, destroys those murderers and burns their
city. That is destruction, stated plainly.

The man without a wedding garment is then bound hand and foot and cast into the outer
darkness, where there is weeping and gnashing of teeth. The whole parable takes place
in the ordinary physical world of kings, servants and cities, and nothing between
verse 7 and verse 13 signals a shift to a realm where a response continues for ever.

That creates a difficulty for reading the phrase as unending conscious torment. It
would mean that those who openly refused the invitation and murdered the king's
servants received the lesser, finite punishment of being destroyed, while the man
who came improperly dressed received an infinitely greater one. Read instead as the
sorrow and anger of being shut out of the feast and ultimately perishing, the two
outcomes fall into a sensible order.

Being bound hand and foot and thrown out points the same way. To be put out of the
feast is to be put away from light, protection and food. A person tied up outside is
not being housed somewhere, and is not going to last long.

## Matthew 25 and the outer darkness

<Scripture reference="Matthew 25:14-30" />

The worthless servant is cast into the outer darkness, and there is weeping and
gnashing of teeth. The same formula appears earlier in Matthew, applied to those
who are thrown out while others sit down at the feast:

<Scripture reference="Matthew 8:12" />

Sorrow and anger expressed in the darkness outside do not imply that the one
expressing them is immortal. And the wording repays exact reading: "there will be" is
not "there will for ever be."

A pattern runs through all these parables. The righteous have somewhere prepared for
them: a barn, containers, a house, a feast. The unrighteous have nowhere. Even being
thrown into the furnace is a way of being thrown away rather than of being placed.
That argument is developed in S20.

## What is never said

The Bible nowhere says there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth for ever and ever.
The nearest thing to it is the torment of Revelation 20:10, stated of the devil, the
beast and the false prophet, which is treated in S03.

## Outer darkness in Jude and 2 Peter

<Scripture reference="Jude 13" />

The phrase about darkness being reserved for ever admits of two readings, and both
should be stated. The first is that the blackest darkness has always been reserved
for them, from the beginning, so that "for ever" describes how long the reservation
has stood rather than how long they will be in it. The second is that their end is
to be for ever in blackest darkness; the author judges this the less likely of the
two on linguistic grounds, and that judgement is his own rather than a finding this
page can verify.

Even on the second reading the verse fits conditional immortality. Non-existence, and
above all being no longer in the presence of the Lord, is the deepest darkness there
is.

<Scripture reference="2 Peter 2:17" />

The author reads this part of 2 Peter as running in chronological order, which would
place the darkness of 2:17 before the Day of the Lord in chapter 3, and so before the
unrighteous are destroyed by fire. That is an inference about the letter's
arrangement, not a claim the passage makes about itself. Earlier in the same chapter,
2 Peter 2:4 describes darkness as the condition of the angels being held for
judgment, so it makes sense that the wicked would experience it before judgment too.

Darkness carries the association elsewhere. It covered the land before Jesus died on
the cross, functioning as a sign of impending death rather than a permanent dwelling.
That passage is treated in S17.

## What this argument establishes

- Gnashing of teeth conveys anger everywhere else it appears in Scripture, and
  weeping conveys grief.
- The phrase occurs both with fire and without any fire, so it is not a
  description of the experience of burning.
- In Luke 13 the only place described in the story is the house, so "in that
  place" most naturally refers to the judgment scene.
- No text says that weeping and gnashing of teeth continues for ever and ever.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That the response is brief, or that it involves no pain. Neither follows.
- That the phrase can never refer to any period after the judgment scene itself.
- That the parables settle the question of final punishment. They are parables,
  and each makes its own point.

<Callout tone="caution" title="What conditionalism is not saying" as="h3">
God is making it very clear in these passages that the punishment leading up to
the second death is no light thing. God does not simply snap his fingers and the
wicked cease to exist. The punishment is real, it is devastating, and it is to be
avoided at all costs. Anyone who takes from this page the impression that
conditional immortality makes the judgment mild has misread it. The disagreement
with the traditional view concerns whether the suffering continues without end,
not whether it is severe.
</Callout>

## Conclusion

Weeping and gnashing of teeth, and the darkness that accompanies it, make sense in
the intermediate state and they make sense as a response to the punishment phase
of the Day of Judgment. Neither location requires that they last for ever.
