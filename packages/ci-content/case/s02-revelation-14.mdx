## In brief

Revelation 14:11 does not say that the torment rises for ever and ever. It says
the smoke of their torment does. That distinction is not a debating trick: rising
smoke is used elsewhere in Scripture as a standing monument to a destruction that
is already finished.

Eternal conscious torment (ECT), the view that the lost suffer consciously
without end, treats this passage as one of the clearest statements of the
doctrine in Scripture. Conditional immortality (CI), the view that unending life
is given to the redeemed rather than possessed by everyone, reads the clause the
way Revelation's own parallels read it. This page sets out the grammar, the two
parallels, and three possible readings of the clause about having no rest, none
of which the author claims to have settled.

## The primary passage

<Scripture reference="Revelation 14:9-11" />

## Why ECT interpreters cite this passage

Taken as a whole, this is a formidable text for the traditional view, and its
force should be felt before any response is offered.

The passage says these people will be tormented with fire and sulphur. It says
this happens in the presence of the holy angels and of the Lamb, which places the
scene in the sight of heaven rather than in some abandoned corner of the
universe. It says the smoke of their torment goes up for ever and ever. And it
says they have no rest day or night. Four elements, stacked in a single sentence,
each of which points naturally toward continuing conscious suffering.

The phrase rendered "for ever and ever" is the strongest expression Revelation
has for endlessness, and Revelation uses it of the life and reign of God himself.
A reader who accepts that the phrase means without end when it is applied to God
is under some pressure to accept that it means without end here.

The argument from smoke is answered directly by ECT interpreters. Smoke is
produced by burning. If smoke is going up for ever, on the ordinary sense of the
words something is burning for ever. On this reading the clause about smoke is
not a softening of the torment but a way of describing it: the smoke goes up for
ever because the burning does.

"No rest day or night" is then read as the plainest element of the four. Rest is
what a sufferer wants and does not receive. The same expression describes torment
in Revelation 20:10, where the phrase is used of the devil, the beast and the
false prophet. The two passages read as mutually reinforcing.

Finally, this passage concerns human beings. Whatever is said about the beast and
the false prophet elsewhere, those who receive the mark and worship the image are
people, which makes Revelation 14 the passage where the traditional view is
applied to humans in the most explicit terms.

## Who the passage addresses

One limit on the passage is set by the passage itself. The warning is addressed
to anyone who worships the beast and its image and receives its mark. It is not a
general statement about the fate of all the unrighteous. Whatever it establishes,
it establishes about that group in the first instance, and extending it to
everyone under judgment is a further step that has to be argued rather than
assumed.

## What is said to rise for ever

The grammatical observation is simple. The verse does not say "they will be
tormented for ever and ever." It says the smoke of their torment rises for ever
and ever. The subject of the enduring is the smoke.

That matters because Scripture uses rising smoke elsewhere, in exactly these
words, for places whose destruction was complete and is over.

### Edom

<Scripture reference="Isaiah 34:9-10" />

The passage says the smoke will go up for ever. It then says what the permanent
condition of the place will be, and it is not perpetual burning. It is desolation:
the land lies waste from generation to generation and no one passes through it.
The everlasting element is the desolation, and the rising smoke is the sign of it.

### Babylon

<Scripture reference="Revelation 19:3" />

The same expression, this time in Revelation itself, and this time about a city
whose destruction has already been announced:

<Scripture reference="Revelation 18:21" />

Babylon is thrown down like a millstone into the sea and will not be found again.
The smoke that goes up for ever and ever belongs to something already destroyed.

One conditionalist study notes that several of the symbols gathered in Revelation
14:9-11 reappear together a few chapters later around the fall of Babylon:
drinking the wine of God's wrath, being tormented with fire, and smoke rising for
ever <Cite id="rethinking-hell-bible-fellowship-church" />. On that reading, the
cluster of images in chapter 14 is Revelation's own vocabulary for a completed
judgment.

### Sodom

The pattern is older than either. After Sodom and Gomorrah were destroyed,
Abraham looked out and saw smoke going up from the plain:

<Scripture reference="Genesis 19:28" />

There was smoke, and the cities were gone. Nothing was still being consumed.

A first-century reader who knew Isaiah and Genesis would be equipped to read
Revelation 14:11 as a description of a judgment so complete and so final that the
smoke of it stands for ever as a memorial. The author of the source document
takes it to be exactly that: a permanent monument to God's perfect judgment of
evil, not something forgotten ten thousand years into eternity.

## The objection: smoke requires something burning

The natural objection has already been stated above: for smoke to be present for
ever, something must be burning for ever.

The author's answer is a question. Isaiah 34 and Revelation 19 both describe
smoke rising for ever from destructions that are complete, so Scripture itself
already uses the expression that way. And if God can sustain a person for ever in
a flame that would otherwise consume them, which is what the traditional view
requires, then causing smoke to continue rising is not a greater difficulty.

<Callout tone="author" title="The author's own qualification" as="h3">
In his own comments on the source document the author is explicit about the limit
of his position. He believes Isaiah's prophecy against Edom has already come to
pass, and that there is not literally smoke still rising from Edom today, while
the destruction of the nation remains complete. That is precisely why he treats
"smoke rising for ever" as a non-literal expression rather than as a claim about
a column of smoke that a traveller could photograph. He is not arguing that
literal smoke ascends somewhere for eternity; he is arguing that Scripture uses
the phrase for a destruction whose result is permanent.
</Callout>

## Torment in the presence of the holy angels and of the Lamb

The passage places the torment in the presence of the holy angels and of the
Lamb. The author observes that if the torment were active for ever, and the angels
and the Lamb were present to it for ever, that would have implications for their
presence elsewhere, since angels are not omnipresent.

This is an observation rather than a proof, and it should be labelled as one. It
depends on a particular view of how angelic presence works and on where the
redeemed are in relation to the scene. Readers who hold different views on either
point will not feel the force of it. It is recorded here because it is part of the
source's argument, not because it settles anything.

## The cup of wrath

One further link in the passage is worth naming. Those who worship the beast
drink the wine of God's fury, poured full strength into the cup of his wrath. The
author's question is a simple one: if Christ died from drinking the cup of God's
wrath, what leads us to expect that the unrighteous will survive drinking it? The
imagery of the cup, wherever else it appears, is not the imagery of an ordeal
survived.

## "No rest day or night": three readings

The clause about having no rest is the hardest part of the passage for the
conditionalist reading, and the source document does not pretend otherwise. It
offers three possibilities and settles on none of them.

The starting point is a verbal parallel. In English translation, Revelation 4:8
and Revelation 14:11 share the same clause about having no rest day and night.
The first is about the living creatures around the throne:

<Scripture reference="Revelation 4:8" />

The living creatures have no rest day and night, and what they are doing without
rest is worship. The expression is therefore not, in Revelation's own usage,
reserved for suffering. It can describe unceasing activity of any kind.

**Reading A.** If the verbal similarity is doing real work, the worshippers of the
beast may be presented as a wicked counterpart to the creatures around the
throne. On that reading the clause describes their state before judgment, while
they are on the earth worshipping the beast without rest, rather than their state
afterwards.

**Reading B.** The clause describes them during their torment, which the rest of
the passage indicates is real, while it lasts. No rest is then a description of
the character of the judgment rather than of its duration.

**Reading C.** The original text carried no punctuation, and the sentence
divisions in modern editions are editorial. On a different division the clause
could attach to the smoke rather than to the people, so that the smoke of their
torment rises for ever and ever and never stops day or night. The smoke would then
function as a kind of everlasting incense in the same way that the worship around
the throne is unceasing.

These are offered as possibilities, not as conclusions. The author does not claim
that any one of them is correct, and this page does not either. What he claims is
narrower: each of the three is compatible with a torment that does not continue
actively for ever, so the clause does not by itself decide the question.

<Callout tone="caution" title="Awaiting specialist review" as="h3">
The source document supported the Revelation 4:8 and 14:11 comparison with two
screenshots of the Greek text. Those images are not reproduced here, and the
parallel is stated above only at the level that can be checked in English
translation, where both verses share the clause about having no rest day and
night. Whether the underlying Greek similarity is close enough to carry the weight
Reading A places on it needs review by someone with formal training in Greek
before this page states it more strongly. Reading C likewise depends on
punctuation and clause division decisions that belong to a textual specialist.
</Callout>

## What this argument establishes

- What the text says rises for ever and ever is the smoke, not the tormenting.
- Rising smoke functions elsewhere in Scripture, including elsewhere in
  Revelation, as a memorial of a destruction that is complete.
- The passage addresses worshippers of the beast specifically, not the
  unrighteous in general.
- The clause about having no rest admits of more than one reading, and at least
  one biblical use of the expression describes unceasing worship rather than
  unceasing suffering.

### What it does not establish by itself

- That the torment described is brief or painless. The passage describes wrath
  poured out at full strength, and nothing here softens that.
- Which of the three readings of the no-rest clause is correct. None is claimed.
- That the traditional reading is impossible. It shows that the passage does not
  compel it.
